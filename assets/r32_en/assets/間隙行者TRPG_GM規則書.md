# Chapter 1: Gap Creatures

> ▶ The complete bestiary of the five Gap Creatures (origin, attributes, liberation conditions, negotiation methods, possible gifts) is in **assets/ 間隙生物圖鑑.md**. Only the rules and resolution tables directly relevant to GM running are retained here.

## §3.3 Gap Creatures — Cannot Be Killed, Only Helped to Pass Over

> Core design principle: Gap Creatures are not monsters. They are **beings trapped in a state of transition**. They have no Hit Points (HP), no Armor Class (AC), no weapon dice — only a Negotiation Difficulty Class (DC) and liberation conditions.

### 3.3.1 The Five Gap Creatures

#### A. Threshold Lingerer

| Item | Content |
|---|---|
| **Origin** | Someone who hesitated too long in the moment of making a major life decision. Now they can never cross that Threshold |
| **Behavior** | Repeatedly walks toward the same door, touches the handle, but never opens it |
| **Attributes** | Anchor 2 (×2 = 4), Negotiation Difficulty Class (DC) **10** |
| **Liberation Condition** | Help them "take that step" — you need to know what they are hesitating about. Gain clues from their memory fragments (Retrieval Difficulty Class (DC) 12) or from Surface investigation (Awareness Difficulty Class (DC) 10) |
| **Liberation Reward** | Gain 1 Return Mark; that Threshold becomes your Memory Threshold from then on (entry Difficulty Class (DC) automatically –3) |

#### B. Lost Item Collector

| Item | Content |
|---|---|
| **Origin** | Was once a Gap Walker — but stayed too long and forgot they belonged to the Surface |
| **Behavior** | Hoards Lost Items, categorizing them by logic even they no longer remember |
| **Attributes** | Retrieval 4 (×2 = 8), Negotiation Difficulty Class (DC) **14** |
| **Weakness** | An irresistible craving for "new Lost Items" |
| **Negotiation Method** | Trade with a Lost Item they have never seen — one you just found in the Gap, or one you brought in from the Surface |
| **Possible Gifts** | Information (location of a Sealed Space Crack), a rare Lost Item from their collection, a Gap Shortcut |

#### C. the Mirror

| Item | Content |
|---|---|
| **Origin** | Not trapped — it is a product of the Gap itself. They are "the reflection in a reflected space" |
| **Behavior** | Mimics your movements. You walk, it walks. You stop, it stops. But sometimes — it does not stop with you |
| **Attributes** | Equal to the full attribute values of whoever encounters it |
| **Negotiation Difficulty Class (DC)** | **16** (Contest Check: your Negotiation vs the Mirror's Negotiation; the Mirror uses the same Negotiation value as you) |
| **Core Rule** | **Never turn your back on the Mirror.** Exit the Gap while watching it — otherwise, next time you enter, it may be waiting at the spot where you last stood |
| **Special Interaction** | If a character has established a special bond with the Mirror (see Ch2 Mirror Sacrifice rules), a special interaction may be triggered. Sacrificing the Mirror can reverse one Gap Sickness stage, at the cost of permanently losing the Mirror |

#### D. Path Guardian

| Item | Content |
|---|---|
| **Origin** | Some large creature trapped by the Gap — a horse that can never cross a bridge, a dog hesitating at a doorway |
| **Behavior** | Blocks an important Gap path. Not hostile — it does not know whether it should let you pass |
| **Attributes** | Traverse 3 (×2 = 6), Negotiation Difficulty Class (DC) **12** |
| **Liberation Condition** | Give it a "reason" to cross — a carrot, a name, or help it find the master it is waiting for |
| **If Liberated** | Gain 1 Return Mark; that path opens to you permanently from then on, and Gap Endurance cost in that area –1 |

#### E. Seal Watcher

| Item | Content |
|---|---|
| **Origin** | A being that stands beside a Sealed Space Crack. Not hostile — it does not know whether it should let you pass |
| **Behavior** | Blocks the Crack, staring at you. Says nothing |
| **Attributes** | Anchor 5 (×2 = 10), Negotiation Difficulty Class (DC) **18** |
| **Pass Condition** | Prove you "need to pass" — show it a Lost Item that must be related to the Sealed Space (gain clues from Surface investigation or a Memory Crystal) |
| **Force Passage** | Difficulty Class (DC) 22 Traverse check. Failure: rejected — that Crack closes to you permanently, and Gap Sickness immediately worsens (check) |

### 3.3.2 Negotiation Exchange Table

Gap Creatures do not use language — they use **"exchange."** Every negotiation is a transaction.

#### What You Can Give:

| Give | Value Tier | Description |
|---|---|---|
| A memory (obtained from a Memory Crystal) | High | Gap Creatures feed on memory. This is your most precious leverage |
| A Lost Item | Mid–High | Depends on the item's emotional weight. The Lost Item Collector especially values "unseen" ones |
| A span of time | Mid | Stay in the Gap for X extra turns to keep it company. What you spend is your Gap Endurance |
| A name | Highest | Call its True Name — must be obtained elsewhere. Once used, it is free |
| A promise | Mid | "I will come back" — if you break your word, that creature's liberation Difficulty Class (DC) becomes +5 for you from then on |
| A story of yours | Low–Mid | Tell a true story about yourself. Beings in the Gap have a natural curiosity about "Surface stories" |

#### What You Can Obtain:

| Obtain | Value Tier | Description |
|---|---|---|
| Pass the path it guards | Mid | A Gap Shortcut — shortens the distance between two points |
| Information | Mid–High | Location of a Sealed Space Crack, whereabouts of another Lost Item, someone's True Name |
| Its assistance | Mid | Help you search for Lost Items (Retrieval Difficulty Class (DC) –5 in that area) or guide you to a specific location |
| A service | High | Obtainable only through exchange by "name." Afterward it is free — you will see it cross that Threshold |
| Return Mark | — | Not obtained through exchange — automatically gained upon liberating it |

#### Negotiation Difficulty Baseline

| Negotiation Difficulty Class (DC) | Description |
|---|---|
| 8 | Exchange value clearly favors it — it will almost certainly accept |
| 12 | Fair exchange — requires a successful check |
| 16 | Exchange disadvantages it — it will hesitate |
| 20 | You ask it to do something against its nature — may refuse, may demand extra cost |

**Negotiation Modifiers**:
- Each Return Mark tier (First Mark / Familiar Mark / Deep Mark / Inscribed Mark / Legendary Mark): +1 to Negotiation checks (see Ch2 §2.9.3)
- Holding a Lost Item related to that creature: +5
- Having broken a promise to that creature before: –5
- Knowing the creature's True Name: +5 and may perform a "Name Exchange"

## §3.4 Damage — Appears as Conditions

> Core design principle: Gap Walkers do not inflict "damage" on Gap Creatures — conflict in the Gap is not based on Hit Points (HP). What characters bear is **Conditions**, not lost Hit Points.

### 3.4.1 Sources of Conflict and Conditions

| Source of Conflict | Consequence | Removal Condition |
|---|---|---|
| Contact with an eroded entity | **Erosion condition**: lose 1d4 maximum Gap Endurance per turn. Can stack | Automatically removed after returning to the Surface; in the Gap requires Difficulty Class (DC) 15 Anchor check |
| the Mirror's active contest | **Resonance condition**: the Gap Endurance you lose, the Mirror also bears an equal amount. And vice versa | Removed when either side's Gap Endurance drops to 0 — but the loser bears Gap Sickness worsening |
| Being Lost (triggered at Critical stage) | **Lost condition**: disadvantage on all orientation checks, cannot mark paths. GM decides your movement | Successfully pass a Difficulty Class (DC) 14 Anchor check (costs 1 Action Point (AP)) |
| Witnessing an Unexplainable Phenomenon | **Stun condition**: lose 1 Action Point (AP) (next turn) | Automatically removed |
| Rejected by a Path Guardian | **Repulsion condition**: that path cannot be attempted again for 24 hours | Wait for time to pass |
| Isolation in a Sealed Space | **Isolation condition**: Anchor check Difficulty Class (DC) +5. You feel the Surface slipping away | Removed after leaving the Sealed Space |

### 3.4.2 Condition Chains

Certain conditions can stack into a Condition Chain — three of the same type lead to permanent consequences:

| Condition Chain | Result after 3 accumulations |
|---|---|
| Erosion ×3 | Maximum Gap Endurance permanently –2 |
| Lost ×3 | GM secretly rolls once for Gap Sickness worsening — does not reveal the result until you return to the Surface |
| Resonance ×3 | the Mirror gains one of your memories. You no longer remember what it was — but it does |

### 3.4.3 No Death Rule

Gap Walkers do not "die" in the Gap — at least not in the traditional sense. When a character bears excessive conditions in the Gap:

- Gap Endurance (GE) depleted → acute Gap Sickness episode → character is Forced Ejected from the Gap (back to the nearest safe Threshold), bearing 1d4 days of Exhaustion
- If a character fails the worsening contest three times in a row at Terminal Gap Sickness stage → the character transforms into a **Lost Item Collector** (NPC, taken over by the GM)

**Gap Walkers do not die. They get lost.** — this is the core of the design.

# Chapter 2: Sealed Space

## §3.5 Sealed Space Risks

### 3.5.1 Sealed Space Characteristics

A Sealed Space is a space "without the Gap" — normal spaces have Cracks, Thresholds, seams between them that Gap Walkers can exploit. A Sealed Space is completely shut:

- Cannot enter from the Gap (the only Crack is Difficulty Class (DC) 22)
- Cannot observe the interior from the Gap
- Things inside never fall into the Gap
- After entering, the Crack may vanish behind you

### 3.5.2 Reasons for Sealing

| Reason | Feature | Clue |
|---|---|---|
| **Deliberately sealed** | The Crack has regular edges — it is artificial | May bear the mark of the Sealer |
| **Space not yet complete** | The Gap around the Crack is "empty" — no distortion, just nothing | New construction, new rooms |
| **Healed after a Gap disaster** | Scar tissue around the Crack — the space self-healed after being torn open too much | Especially many Gap Creatures in that area |
| **Something is locked inside** | Defensive seals around the Crack — not to prevent intrusion, but to prevent escape | Patrolling perception-type Gap Creatures near the Crack |

### 3.5.3 Opening Methods and Risks

| Method | Difficulty | Risk |
|---|---|---|
| **Find the Sealed Crack** | Awareness Difficulty Class (DC) 22 | The Crack may vanish after you enter — you are trapped in the Sealed Space |
| **Create a trauma event on the Surface** | Medium (narrative difficulty) | The event itself has consequences — would you set a fire to open a Sealed Space? |
| **Find the key left by the Sealer** | Special plot | The key may be a Lost Item, a Memory Crystal, or the Sealer themselves |
| **Open with a Gap Construct** | Random (Retrieval Difficulty Class (DC) 18 to obtain a Gap Construct) | The sole purpose of some Gap Constructs is to open specific Sealed Spaces — but you cannot know in advance |
| **Persuade the Seal Watcher** | Negotiation Difficulty Class (DC) 18 | Must present a Lost Item related to the Sealed Space (see §3.3.1-E) |

### 3.5.4 Rules Inside a Sealed Space

- Base Gap Endurance cost doubles (–2/turn)
- Cannot exit to the Gap — can only leave from the Surface (if the building has a Surface exit)
- If the building has no exit: you must create a new Threshold from within (Anchor Difficulty Class (DC) 20, costs 3 Action Points (AP))
- Time flow inside may be ten times the Surface, or one-tenth (GM secretly rolls d6)

## §3.6 Gap Sickness System (Contest-Based)

### 3.6.1 Gap Sickness Stages

| Stage | Trigger Condition | Symptoms |
|---|---|---|
| **Early Stage** | First failed worsening contest after Gap Endurance (GE) is depleted | Occasionally "see" the Gap while on the Surface — Thresholds glowing, corridors longer than they are. Awareness check passively triggered (you cannot turn it off) |
| **Middle Stage** | Second failed worsening contest | the Surface and the Gap begin to blur — you do not know which layer you are on. The GM no longer tells you |
| **Late Stage** | Third failed worsening contest | You begin to turn translucent — Surface people sometimes "see through you to the wall behind." Social disadvantage. The dying can see you |
| **Terminal Stage** | Fourth failed worsening contest | You become a Gap Creature. Character taken over by the GM — becomes a new Lost Item Collector NPC |

> **Last chance before Terminal transformation**: see §3.6.4 Terminal Final Check (Difficulty Class (DC) 22).

### 3.6.2 Worsening Contest (after numeric rescaling)

```
Gap Sickness worsening contest: d20 + Anchor  vs  Anchor × 2 + Traverse
```

> **Formula note**: The contest target value = Anchor × 2 + Traverse. This represents Gap Sickness as a contest against oneself — your Anchor (your link to reality) against a combination of your Anchor and Traverse (your "depth" in the Gap). The higher your Traverse, the more at ease you are in the Gap — but the more at ease, the deeper you are trapped.

| Character Example | d20 + Anchor | vs | Anchor×2 + Traverse | Success Rate |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Anchor 8, Traverse 3 | d20 + 8 | vs | 16 + 3 = **19** | Need to roll 11+ (50%) |
| Anchor 5, Traverse 5 | d20 + 5 | vs | 10 + 5 = **15** | Need to roll 10+ (55%) |
| Anchor 10, Traverse 6 | d20 + 10 | vs | 20 + 6 = **26** | Need to roll 16+ (25%) |
| Anchor 3, Traverse 8 | d20 + 3 | vs | 6 + 8 = **14** | Need to roll 11+ (50%) |

> **Design intent**: The strongest Gap Walkers — high Anchor, high Traverse — face the highest contest difficulty. This reflects the game's core tension: the more powerful you are in the Gap, the closer you are to becoming part of it.

### 3.6.3 Sources of Advantage in the Worsening Contest

| Source of Advantage | Trigger Condition | Type |
|---|---|---|
| Holding an Anchoring Item | Passive — takes effect simply by holding | Ch3 / Ch2 §2.5 |
| Touching an Anchoring Item | Active — costs 1 Action Point (AP), grants advantage on all Anchor checks (including the worsening contest) that turn | Ch2 §2.5.2 |
| 3+ Return Marks (Familiar Mark) | Passive — only for **Early Stage** Gap Sickness worsening contests | Ch2 §2.9.3 |
| 5+ Return Marks (Deep Mark) | Passive — for **Middle Stage and below** Gap Sickness worsening contests | Ch2 §2.9.3 |

> Advantages do not stack (standard d20 system rule: multiple advantages still only roll twice and take the higher).

### 3.6.4 Terminal Final Check

When a character enters Terminal Stage (fourth failed worsening contest), before transforming into a Gap Creature, there is one last chance:

```
Terminal Final Check: Difficulty Class (DC) 22 Anchor check (advantage when holding an Anchoring Item)
Success → Terminal reverts to Late Stage. Character is Forced Ejected from the Gap, bearing 1d6 days of Exhaustion.
Failure → Transformation is irreversible. Character becomes a Lost Item Collector.
```

- **Only one chance.** Cannot retry, cannot be assisted by others
- This is not ordinary treatment — it is the final Threshold

### 3.6.5 Treatment Methods (unified table)

Fully aligned with Ch2 §2.11.1:

| # | Treatment Method | Effect | Limit | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Surface Rest** | 7 consecutive days without entering the Gap, Difficulty Class (DC) 10 Anchor daily, 7 successes → Early Stage removed | Entering the Gap during this resets the count | Merged |
| 2a | **First finding of Anchoring Item** | Middle Stage → Early Stage (one-time narrative milestone) | Triggers only once | Ch2 |
| 2b | **Continuously holding an Anchoring Item** | Gap Endurance (GE) max +5, advantage on worsening contests, slows perception of endurance cost | Interrupted if Anchoring Item is Worn/lost | Ch3 |
| 3 | **Helping a Gap Creature pass over** | Reverts one stage below Late Stage | Requires successfully liberating the creature (see §3.3.1) | Consistent |
| 4 | **Return Mark protection** | 3 Marks → advantage on Early Stage worsening; 5 Marks → advantage on Middle Stage and below worsening | Passive | Merged |
| 5a | **Another's Anchor assistance** | Any Walker assisting → advantage on next worsening contest (short-term) | Difficulty Class (DC) 14 Anchor | Ch3 |
| 5b | **Master healing** | Walker with 7+ Return Marks assisting → Middle Stage → Early Stage | 8-hour ritual | Ch2 |
| 6 | **Terminal Final Check** | Difficulty Class (DC) 22, success → Terminal → Late Stage; failure → irreversible transformation | Only once | Ch3 |
| 7 | **Mirror Sacrifice** | Reverts one stage, permanently loses the Mirror | Requires a special bond with the Mirror | Ch2 |

## §3.7 Gap Physical Anomalies

### 3.7.1 Distance Distortion — Relational Distance

The distance between two points in the Gap does not depend on physical space, but on the **relationship** between those two points on the Surface:

| Relationship Type | Gap Distance | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Blood-tied | Extremely near (reachable in 1 turn) | Grandmother's room ⇄ granddaughter's room in an ancestral old house |
| Emotional resonance | Near (reachable in 2 turns) | Where a confession happened in this classroom ⇄ where the breakup happened in that classroom |
| Functionally related | Medium (reachable in 3 turns) | Kitchen ⇄ dining room |
| History overlapping | Far (reachable in 4 turns) | Two buildings on the same street that were the same factory 50 years ago |
| Unrelated | Extremely far (5+ turns or unreachable) | Two random modern office buildings |

> The GM must annotate 1–3 relational clues for each important location when building a scene, for players to infer the Shortcut in the Gap.

### 3.7.2 Directional Tilt

The "up" in the Gap is not necessarily the "up" of the Surface:

- Failure on a Traverse check may cause your position or orientation to be abnormal when exiting the Gap. Roll d6 to determine the deviation:

| d6 | Deviation Type | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| 1–2 | Displacement | Exit position off by 1d10 meters (random direction) |
| 3–4 | Orientation scramble | Up/down, left/right inverted, lasts 1d4 turns (disadvantage on all Traverse checks) |
| 5–6 | Time shift | Surface elapsed time ×1d4 (stacks on top of time flow) |
- Staying long in the Gap (Gap Endurance (GE) < 25%) — sense of direction begins to collapse. GM may require a Traverse Difficulty Class (DC) 12 to confirm direction
- In a Gravity Anomaly Zone, direction fails completely — movement costs double Action Points (AP)

### 3.7.3 Time-Flow Anomaly

Roll the Time-Flow table each time you exit the Gap:

| d6 | Surface Elapsed Time | Description |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1 minute | You came out almost the instant you entered — onlookers only see you briefly space out |
| 2 | Same as in the Gap | Time corresponds normally |
| 3 | Same as in the Gap | Time corresponds normally |
| 4 | Double the time | You are a few hours later than expected — the client starts to worry |
| 5 | Five times the time | Half a day has passed. Your phone has a pile of missed calls |
| 6 | One-tenth the time | You spent hours in the Gap, but only minutes passed on the Surface — your body has not caught up |

> The GM may override the roll at key plot points. The main use of time flow is to create narrative pressure and unexpected consequences.

### 3.7.4 Gravity Anomaly Zone

Some Gap regions have no gravity — objects and people float:

- Identify a Gravity Anomaly Zone: Awareness Difficulty Class (DC) 10 (the boundary of the anomaly is usually visible — light bends there)
- Movement in a Gravity Anomaly Zone: Traverse Difficulty Class (DC) 12; on failure you drift in a random direction
- Endurance cost: –2/turn (additional, on top of base cost)
- Benefit: certain Lost Items **only linger in Gravity Anomaly Zones** — they are "things stuck too long between two spaces"

## §3.8 Quick Reference — GM Encounter Flow

### Gap Exploration Turn Flow

```
1. GM describes the current Gap scene (visual, sound, air quality)
2. Players declare this turn's 2 Action Point (AP) allocation
3. Resolve actions (checks, movement, negotiation)
4. GM informs of endurance cost
5. If endurance crosses a stage threshold (50% / 25% / 10% / 0) → trigger that stage's effect
6. If turn count reaches the encounter judgment point → GM secretly rolls the encounter judgment (d20)
7. Next turn
```

### Encounter Judgment Table

Roll d20 every 3 Gap turns or upon entering a new area:

| d20 | Result |
|---|---|
| 1–5 | Threat encounter (eroded-entity fragments, hostile Mirror) |
| 6–12 | Neutral encounter (Gap Creature, strange phenomenon, dropped item) |
| 13–18 | Favorable encounter (clue, shortcut, usable Lost Item) |
| 19–20 | Safe (no encounter, and Gap Endurance cost in that area –1 this turn) |

**Modifiers**:
- Holding an Anchoring Item: +1
- Gap Sickness at Middle Stage or worse: –3
- Holder of 5+ Return Marks: +2
- Each layer deeper into the Gap Layer: –2

# Chapter 4: World & Narrative

### 4.1.2 the Gap Layer: the Back of the World

#### What is the Gap?

The Gap was not "created" — it was **accumulated**.

Every time something is lost, a moment forgotten, a decision left hanging in hesitation — a tiny crease forms in the fabric of reality. Billions of creases, over thousands of years, accumulated into the Gap.

Imagine the Surface world as one giant cloth. The Gap is not another space beneath the cloth — it is the **thickness** of the cloth itself. Normally, that thickness is negligible. But when creases pile up, threads come loose, warp and weft interweave… the thickness becomes an enterable space.

#### the "Geography" of the Gap

The Gap has no geography of its own — it is a **distorted mirror** of Surface geography.

- **Correspondence**: every place in the Gap "corresponds" to somewhere on the Surface. But the correspondence is not one-to-one — a Surface intersection may split into three different junctions in the Gap, a Surface room may extend infinitely in the Gap.
- **Density difference**: the Gap has different "depth" in different regions. Spaces used frequently by humans, full of clear meaning (such as a busy office), are "thin" in the Gap. Forgotten, transitional spaces (abandoned buildings, underground passages, shut-down shopping streets) are "thick" in the Gap.
- **Gap Abyss**: at some extremely ancient overlays of forgetting (the bottom of old cities, submerged settlements, battlefields never recorded), the Gap becomes extremely deep. Walkers call it the "Deep Gap" — even veterans dare not enter lightly.

#### the Origin of the Gap (GM background knowledge)

The following are not facts the player characters can certainly know. This is an internal-consistency tool for the GM when building the world. Choose the one that best fits your group's tone — or mix them.

**Theory One: Sedimentation (default)**
The Gap is a byproduct of consciousness. Every self-aware being leaves a "fingerprint of consciousness" in reality. When attention moves away from a place, the fingerprint remains but the owner leaves — this is the raw material of the Gap. This explains why the Gap is deepest in densely human areas (most conscious activity), and why Gap Creatures seem to have residue "intent."

**Theory Two: Creation Residue**
When the universe was born, not all space was "completed." The Gap is filler from the creation process — like the rubble a sculptor leaves around a stone statue. This explains the existence of Sealed Spaces (creation residue never opened) and a certain "non-human" quality of the Gap.

**Theory Three: the Drainage System of Forgetting**
This view holds that the Gap is a cosmic mechanism — recycling the "possibilities" discarded by reality. Every untaken road, every unspoken word, every unchosen option flows into the Gap. The Gap is not a flaw, but a function. This theory implies the Gap has some "purpose" — Sealed Spaces exist to "isolate dangerous possibilities."

**Theory Four: Walker-Creation (fringe belief)**
A few Gap Walkers believe: the Gap only began to exist when the first Gap Walker "discovered" it. Before that, forgetting was just forgetting, losing was just losing. It was observation that gave the Gap its form. This is the most unsettling theory to Walkers — because it means every time they traverse, they make the Gap more "real."

> **GM hint**: choose one theory as the "truth" of your campaign — but never tell the players directly. Let different NPCs propose different theories. The best world-building lets players piece it together themselves.

### 4.1.3 the Gap's "Rules"

The Gap has no natural laws — but it has **patterns**. These patterns are not laws, more like "the Gap's habits." The GM may break them, but breaking them should be meaningful.

#### Rules of Thresholds

**Why do Thresholds have different types?**

A Threshold is the "membrane" between the Surface and the Gap. The texture of the membrane depends on the "cause" of its formation:

| Threshold Type | Formation Cause | Membrane Texture | Emotional Resonance |
|---------|---------|---------|---------|
| **Natural Threshold** | Space itself naturally thins under specific conditions | Stable, predictable, slowly opening and closing | Anticipation in silence |
| **Artificial Threshold** | Human activity repeatedly "wears" reality at the same spot | Rough, sometimes unstable, varies by person | Industrial indifference |
| **Trauma Threshold** | A strong emotional event tears reality | Sharp, dangerous, carries an "echo" | Residue of pain, anger, despair |
| **Memory Threshold** | Collective memory long overlaid at the same spot | Translucent, warm or cold, has imagery | Nostalgia, regret, the unfinished |
| **Sealed Crack** | A Threshold deliberately or naturally sealed | Impermeable, cold, covered by "scar tissue" | Repressed fear, something that should not be opened |

**Common laws of Thresholds**:

1. **Thresholds need "in-between"**: a Threshold never appears at the center of a space. They are on boundaries — between door and wall, at a staircase landing, in the gap of an elevator door, at the seam of shadow between two streetlamps.
2. **Thresholds are diluted by attention**: when too many people notice the same Threshold, it "thins" or even temporarily closes. This is one reason Gap Walkers stay hidden — displaying a Threshold publicly destroys it.
3. **Thresholds remember who used them**: a Walker who repeatedly uses the same Threshold builds a kind of "relationship" with it. Old Thresholds become easier to pass, but also exert a certain gravity on the Walker — you always want to return there.

#### Rules of Lost Items

**Why do things fall into the Gap?**

Not all Lost Items enter the Gap. For an item to "fall," one of the following must be met:

1. **Emotional charging**: the item was given enough emotional meaning by its owner (not valuable — meaningful). An ordinary key, having accompanied someone for twenty years, becomes "heavy" enough to pierce reality.
2. **Lost in "between"**: the item is lost in a transitional state — a pocket on a journey, a cardboard box while moving, a drawer awaiting categorization. Things between two definite states most easily slip into the Gap.
3. **Deliberately discarded yet not forgotten**: a discarded thing, if still remembered, exists in two states at once — physically thrown away, but not let go of in the heart. This contradiction produces enough "crease."
4. **Gravity of collective memory**: when enough people remember the "sense of existence" of something (not necessarily a concrete item), its counterpart may be generated in the Gap. This explains why the Gap has things that look ancient yet belong to no era.

**Special rules of Benevolent Keepsakes**:

Benevolent Keepsakes differ from Common Lost Items — they are not "dropped" but "placed." When a person deliberately leaves an item somewhere "for someone who might need it," this intent forms a layer of protection on the item. Benevolent Keepsakes do not degrade in the Gap, and sometimes even subtly "move" toward the person who needs them. This is the most inexplicable yet most cherished phenomenon to Gap Walkers.

### 4.1.4 the Gap Walker Community

#### How many are there?

No one knows exactly. Most Walkers estimate: **about 500–1000 Gap Walkers worldwide who can actively traverse**, plus roughly three times as many "Sensitives" who can perceive the Gap but cannot (or will not) traverse.

This number maintains a certain stability — the rate at which new Walkers awaken roughly equals the rate at which old Walkers disappear.

#### How does one become a Gap Walker?

No one chooses to become a Gap Walker. The most common "awakening" paths:

1. **Near-death crossing (~40%)**: at the brink of life and death, the soul briefly slips into the Gap and returns. After returning, you can never "unsee."
2. **Inherited awakening (~25%)**: one parent is a Walker. Not genetic — you absorbed too much of the Gap's "presence" growing up, until one day you could see too.
3. **Loss trigger (~20%)**: experiencing a major loss (a beloved's death, loss of all property, being utterly forgotten), the rift of reality opens before you.
4. **Location trigger (~10%)**: spending too much time in specific liminal spaces (night-shift guards, underground parking attendants, long-haul truck drivers), until the space begins to show you its other side.
5. **Unknown (~5%)**: some Walkers simply do not know why — they woke up one day and saw. This is the most unsettling type.

#### How do they recognize each other?

Gap Walkers have no business cards, no LinkedIn, no annual convention. Their recognition is extremely unique:

- **Traces in the Gap**: experienced Walkers leave subtle marks in the Gap — not graffiti (too obvious), but "tidying" a corner, arranging Lost Items into a specific pattern, leaving a Benevolent Keepsake near a Threshold. Other Walkers recognize these are not random.
- **Eyes on the Surface**: when two Walkers meet on the Surface, they may notice how the other looks at door seams — a special "confirmation," not from fear but from recognition.
- **Introduction by Sensitives**: "Sensitives" who cannot traverse but can perceive sometimes act as go-betweens. They run antiquarian bookstores, pawnshops, late-night cafés — these become informal contact points.
- **Veteran guiding newcomer**: most Walkers are "found" by another Walker. Veteran Walkers notice the anomalies around a freshly awakened one — Lost Items frequently appearing nearby, unusual reactions to liminal spaces.

#### Is there organization?

**No formal organization.** The reason is simple:

1. **Too large and visible an organization attracts attention** — and the Gap needs no attention to thin
2. **Walkers have innate distrust of authority** — they have seen too many "rules" lose meaning in the Gap
3. **Gathering accelerates Gap Sickness** — too many Walkers in one place makes the Gap "take notice"

But there are **informal networks**:

| Network Type | Scale | Example |
|---------|------|------|
| **City node** | 3–8 people | "Shibuya group" — five Walkers in Tokyo exchange information at a late-night ramen shop |
| **Mentor chain** | 2–5 people, cross-generation | Veteran Walker → awakened one → awakened one's apprentice; three generations may never meet at once |
| **Commission network** | Fluid | A paid commission system brokered through Sensitives. Anonymous, one-time, untraceable |
| **Guardian** | 1–3 people | Walkers who voluntarily watch specific Sealed Spaces. Lonely work; some have watched for decades |
| **Schism faction** | Defunct | Historically some tried to organize. All failed. Some "failures" remain sealed in Sealed Spaces |

#### the Walkers' Unwritten Rules

These are not laws — but Walkers who violate them usually do not end well:

1. **Do not bring ordinary people into the Gap.** Not to protect them — to protect reality.
2. **Do not leave your name in the Gap.** A name is the anchor of self-awareness. Leave it in the Gap, and the Gap begins to know you.
3. **Do not open a door you do not intend to close.** A Sealed Space was sealed for a reason.
4. **If you see another Walker lost in the Gap — help them.** Today it is them, tomorrow it may be you.
5. **Do not traverse the same Threshold more than three times in a row.** The Threshold will begin to "expect" you back.

#### the Walkers' Daily Life

Becoming a Gap Walker is not a full-time job. Most Walkers have a Surface "cover life":

- Night-shift work (security, convenience store, logistics center) — gives you reason to be active late at night
- Freelance — flexible schedule lets you chase Thresholds
- Antique dealer / secondhand bookstore owner — perfect cover for contacting Lost Items
- Urban explorer / photographer — reasonable excuse to go where you should not
- Unemployed / drifter — the most honest choice, though the hardest to sustain

A few Walkers live off commissions — helping ordinary people recover Lost Items (under the name of "private detective"), handling events where Gap Creatures spill onto the Surface, watching or repairing fragile Sealed Spaces.

## 4.2 Gap Layer Depth Settings

### 4.2.1 Visual & Sensory Description

This section is the GM's **description toolbox**. Each category offers multiple options for different scene tones.

#### Light

The light in the Gap has no clear source. It is not sunlight, not lamp light, more like —

- Everything glows faintly, but you cannot see the source
- Like a cloudy afternoon, forever grey-white
- Shadows longer than they should be, and inconsistent in direction
- Some areas are "too dark" — not a lack of light, but light unwilling to enter
- Always a faint halo of uncertain color in the distance — but you can never reach it
- An item's shadow is sometimes more "real" than the item itself

> **Description example**: "You stand in a corridor with no lamps. Yet you can see — as if someone turned 'visibility' to its lowest brightness. Your shadow falls on three different walls, each pointing a different way."

#### Sound

- Absolute silence — but you hear your own heartbeat, blood flow, the faint creak of bone
- Sounds from afar you cannot locate: metal striking, a door closing, whispers, footsteps
- The delay of sound is strange — your footstep sounds half a second late
- Some places have a persistent low-frequency hum, as if resonating in your chest
- The Gap's "wind" has no source, but you hear it pass through windows that do not exist
- Lost Items sometimes emit faint sounds — a watch ticking, a music-box melody, a voice calling a name

> **Description example**: "It is quiet here. Not the quiet of 'no one is speaking' — but the quiet of 'sound itself forgot this place.' Your footstep lands on the floor, but the sound seems to come from the next room."

#### Smell

- The smell of dust — but the dust itself does not exist
- Ozone, pre-rain air, damp concrete
- Old paper, dry wood, cooled wax
- Specific areas carry a smell that does not match the scene — a flower shop in the Gap may smell of sea breeze
- An unidentifiable smell that evokes strong nostalgia
- Near Sealed Spaces there is "odorless" — like a negative space of smell

> **Description example**: "This corridor smells like a classroom after elementary school lets out. Chalk dust, wooden desks, and some smell you cannot name but instantly recalls being eight years old."

#### Touch

- The air is denser than the Surface, like moving through warm syrup
- The air in some areas is "grainy" — a tiny presence felt on the skin
- An item's surface temperature is wrong — metal may be warm, cloth may be cold
- The Gap's "wind" brushing the skin feels like a forgotten touch
- Gravity does not vanish — it becomes "absent-minded." Sometimes you feel gently pushed in a direction

> **Description example**: "You reach out to touch the wall. It should be cold — this is an underground parking garage. But it is warm. Not just warm, but the warmth of 'someone leaned here not long ago.' But no one is here."

#### Spatial Perception

This is the hardest dimension to describe, yet the most essential trait of the Gap:

- **Unstable perspective**: the far end of a corridor may be larger than the near end — or smaller. Not a perspective illusion, but real.
- **Defamiliarized familiar space**: you recognize this as the corridor of your apartment building. But something is wrong — the curve of the corner, the distance between doors, the texture of the ceiling. As if someone fine-tuned everything while you were not looking.
- **Sense of "thickness" of space**: some areas make you feel space itself is "thick" — like passing through an invisible membrane. Long stays cause headaches.
- **Manifestation of negative space**: in the Gap, "empty space" itself is almost visible — like heat-haze distortion in air, like something beneath the water's surface.

> **Description example**: "This is the corridor you walk every day. But tonight it is about three meters longer than yesterday. Not a hallucination — you counted the steps. And the wall color is slightly off. Not changed color, but… as if the color itself hesitates."

### 4.2.2 Narrative Presentation of Distance Distortion

Fixed distance is the Gap's most core yet hardest-to-handle trait. The following are not math formulas — they are narrative tools.

#### the "Relational Law" of Distance

In the Gap, the distance between two points depends on their **relationship** on the Surface, not physical distance:

| Relationship Type | Distance Effect | Narrative Example |
|---------|---------|---------|
| **Same-type space** | Distance shortened | All "abandoned locker rooms" may be only a few steps apart |
| **Shared memory** | Distance shortened | A Shortcut appears between two places remembered by the same people |
| **Causal connection** | Distance unstable | Between a crime scene and the killer's hideout — distance fluctuates with time |
| **Emotional opposition** | Distance lengthened | Place of happy memory and place of painful memory — may be far apart in the Gap |
| **No relation** | Distance random | Two completely unrelated places — distance may be any number |

#### Three Narrative Modes of Distance Distortion

**Mode One: Maze**

The same corridor walked three times, each time showing a different number of doors. The space beyond the corner should not exist — it is not on the building's floor plan.

> **When to use**: suspense scenes, when players are seeking a specific target but direction is unclear

**Mode Two: Shortcut**

Pass through a room in the Gap, and returning to the Surface you find you have crossed half the city. Experienced Walkers exploit this — but Shortcuts are not stable.

> **When to use**: when you need to move the plot quickly, showing both the usefulness and the unreliability of the Gap

**Mode Three: Extension**

A corridor is only ten meters on the Surface. In the Gap, it extends… very far. No end in sight. Footsteps begin to echo. How long have you walked?

> **When to use**: tense scenes, showing the Gap's danger, accelerating Gap Sickness onset

#### GM Practical Tool: Relational Map

When preparing an adventure, rather than drawing a precise Gap map (impossible), draw a **relational map**:

```
[Abandoned factory entrance]
    |--- "accident that once happened" --- [factory basement] (distance: very near)
    |--- "boss's escape route" --- [safe house at the other end of the city] (distance: sometimes near, sometimes far)
    |--- "workers' nostalgic hometown" --- [village 300 km away] (distance: very far but reachable)
    |--- "unrelated to this place" --- [random location] (distance: unpredictable)
```

Each relational line is annotated with an **emotional/causal keyword** that determines distance tendency. Players can "unlock" relationships by gathering clues — for example, discovering the truth of the factory accident lets them use the accident relationship's Shortcut.

### 4.2.3 Threshold Types: Scene Description Examples

#### Natural Threshold

**Nature**: space naturally thins under specific conditions. The oldest Threshold type.
**Features**: usually located at nature's "in-betweens" — coastline (between sea and land), cave entrance (between mountain and sky), in fog (between visible and invisible), at a specific moment (at day-night transition).

> **Scene description**:
> At low tide, the sea cave entrance is exposed for about two hours. The air inside is different — not damp, but dry, like old book pages. The sound of waves becomes strange deep in the cave — not quieter, but… delayed. As if the waves break somewhere else, and the sound reaches your ears through a long tunnel.
>
> Salt crystals on the cave walls, but arranged too neatly. Not a natural pattern — as if someone wrote something on the wall and then erased it. At the deepest part of the cave — an hour and a half left after low tide — you see a Crack in the rock face. Not split rock, but the space between rocks splitting open.

#### Artificial Threshold

**Nature**: human activity repeatedly wears reality. The most common Threshold type.
**Features**: appears in spaces frequently passed through by humans — a specific turn in the subway (commuter crowds passing repeatedly), a specific hospital corridor (transition between life and death), between specific bookshelves in a library (transition between reading and reality).

> **Scene description**:
> Twenty minutes after the last subway train leaves. The platform is empty. But beside the yellow line at the third car's position, the air trembles faintly — not heat haze, but like someone just passed through a bead curtain and it is still swaying. If you stand there, you smell something not of the subway: old newspaper, coffee, some aged perfume. Then you notice one tile at the platform edge has a slightly wrong color — a bit greyer than those around it, as if it exists in two different eras at once.

#### Trauma Threshold

**Nature**: strong emotion tears reality. The most dangerous Threshold type.
**Features**: does not fully "heal" within a month. An old Trauma Threshold may be re-torn by similar emotion. The surrounding space has an "echo" — sounds, images, fragments of emotion replay repeatedly.

> **Scene description**:
> This intersection is normal by day. But at 2:47 a.m. — the exact time of the accident — the Crack in the asphalt begins to seep something that is not water. The air grows heavy, filled with the taste of metal. You stand exactly at the impact point, and feel in your chest a pain not your own — what the motorcyclist felt three years ago for the last time. At the edge of vision, yellow warning lights begin to flash — but this intersection has no warning lights at all. They flash on the Gap's side.

#### Memory Threshold

**Nature**: collective memory long overlaid. The "warmest" yet most disorienting Threshold.
**Features**: usually located where "many people remember at once" — an old school, a cinema before demolition, an old neighborhood about to be rebuilt. Translucent — you can see the "memory scene" on the other side overlaid on reality.

> **Scene description**:
> This old cinema is to be demolished next week. But tonight, seat 7 in row 11 of the screening hall — where someone spent the two happiest hours of their life — glows faintly. If you sit in that seat, you see two images at once: the peeling wall before you, and another translucent layer of an old film playing on the screen. The two images overlap, like a double exposure. You can smell popcorn — from thirty years ago. If you close your eyes, you hear that film's dialogue, and someone's laughter.

#### Sealed Crack

**Nature**: a deliberately sealed Threshold. The most unsettling type.
**Features**: the surface looks perfectly normal — which is exactly the problem. The surrounding space is "too normal" — no dust, no wear, no trace of time. Approaching it you feel repulsion — not fear, but a definite, physical "you do not want to be here" feeling.

> **Scene description**:
> Basement level 2 of the hospital, the storage room at the end of the corridor. The door plate reads "Archive B," but the font is wrong — this font was never used in this hospital. The handle is clean — too clean, not a single scratch, not a trace of grease. When your hand reaches for the handle, the hairs on your arm stand up — not from cold. The air around the door is still. Not windless, but the air is unwilling to approach this door. If you press your ear to the door — you would not do this, but if you did — you would hear no sound on the other side. Not silence. Void.

### 4.2.4 Unexplainable Phenomena List

The following are **random details** the GM can insert into any Gap scene. They do not explain, do not advance the plot, do not give clues — they exist purely to make the Gap feel "wrong." Use 1–2 per scene.

#### Minor Anomalies (may be used frequently)

1. A mirror reflects a room not entirely the same as the one you are in — an extra window, furniture slightly shifted
2. A row of wet footprints on the floor, but they vanish after only three steps — no origin, no destination
3. All the clocks in a room stopped at the same time — not on the hour, but 4:37
4. The view outside the window is right, but the season is wrong — it is snowing outside, yet it is July
5. A gust of wind through the corridor carries a clearly identifiable fragment of music that does not belong here
6. The font of a sign changes when you look at it a second time
7. You hear someone call your name in the distance — in your elementary-school teacher's tone
8. Chairs and tables in a room are arranged like a meeting setup, with a cup of still-steaming coffee on the table
9. An elevator button has a symbol you have never seen — not a floor number, not a letter
10. The light in a corner comes from the wrong angle — the shadow falls toward the light source

#### Moderate Anomalies (2–3 per scenario)

11. A door in a corridor, once opened, leads to a physically impossible space — a field inside a building
12. All electronic devices simultaneously show the same image — a blurred, unrecognizable face
13. The wallpaper pattern subtly changes as your gaze moves — and restores when you look directly
14. You see the same item in three different rooms — the same scratch, the same stain
15. The number of steps on a flight of stairs differs every time you walk it
16. You see your own back — your doppelgänger turns the corner ten meters ahead of you
17. The air suddenly fills with a definite emotion — sadness, anger, or joy that is not yours
18. The whole room tilts slightly — not an earthquake, but space itself tilted about a degree
19. The shadow on the wall moves, but the corresponding object does not
20. After walking a long way, you find yourself back at the start — but your footprints show you walked straight the whole time

#### Deep Anomalies (at most 1 per scenario)

21. You see a person walk past at the other end of a corridor — but that person's clothing, gait, are exactly like your father's. Your father never came to this city
22. The ceiling disappears. Not "the ceiling is very high" — there is no ceiling. Above is overcast sky. But you are on basement level 3
23. All sound suddenly vanishes — then you hear your own heartbeat, but it does not come from your chest, but from the other end of the corridor
24. You enter a door that should be the next room — and walk into your own childhood bedroom. Everything is there: posters, bedsheets, the tree outside the window. But there is no street outside — only more rooms
25. You find a full-length mirror. The you in the mirror wears different clothes, has scars on the face you do not have. The you in the mirror is also looking at you — and looks more frightened than you
## 4.3 Gap Creature Narrative Guide

### Design Philosophy

Gap Creatures are not "monsters." They do not exist to be defeated. Each Gap Creature is an **embodiment** of some facet of the Gap — their behavior has internal logic, their "hostility" is often defense, confusion, hunger, or an incomprehensible attempt at communication.

When designing an encounter, remember this priority:
1. **Understanding**: can the players understand why this creature is here, what it wants?
2. **Choice**: can the players make a meaningful choice (negotiate / bypass / help / contest)?
3. **Consequence**: what does this encounter change?

---

### 4.3.1 Threshold Lingerer

#### Nature

A Threshold Lingerer was once human — a Gap Walker. In some traversal they "failed to fully return." Not death — but trapped inside the Threshold itself, neither on the Surface nor in the Gap, forever in the process of passing through.

#### Deep Background

Every Threshold Lingerer once had a name, a life, a reason that made them a Gap Walker. Their lingering is not a curse — it is confusion. They do not know where they are, how long they have been like this. They are trapped in the instant of "about to enter" — forever about to cross the Threshold, never crossing it.

Some Lingerers chose to stay — they watch over something in the Threshold: a Sealed Space, a dangerous Threshold only they know, or a young Walker not yet awakened but about to be.

#### Appearance

- Human silhouette, but edges blurred — like an overexposed photograph
- Trails afterimages when moving, as if existing in multiple instants at once
- At the "face" you can sometimes see traces of features — an instant when you see a pair of terrified eyes, a mouth speaking
- Clothing vague — maybe a coat, a uniform, or any ordinary garment you might see in any era
- Faint "Threshold effect" around them — air trembling slightly, abnormal temperature

#### Behavior Patterns

- Paces repeatedly near a specific Threshold — usually the one where they disappeared
- Shows wariness or confusion toward Walkers approaching the Threshold
- Sometimes tries to "communicate" with a Walker — voice sounds like through very thick glass
- Does not attack proactively, but may unconsciously defend when disturbed
- Occasionally "guides" a Walker — points out a dangerous Threshold, or marks a safe path with afterimages

#### Roleplay Hints

| Dimension | Guidance |
|------|------|
| **Tone** | Hollow, distant, broken — like a radio between two channels. Not the low menace of a horror film, but closer to confusion and exhaustion |
| **Movement** | Slow, discontinuous — they jump between two instants. Sometimes "fast-forwards" a movement, then returns to slow |
| **Emotional core** | **Confusion and waiting**. They are not angry ghosts, but lost people. The saddest moment is when players realize the one the Lingerer waits for will never come |

#### Example Encounter Scene

**Scene: the last watchman of the underpass**

*Location*: a forgotten section of the city's underground passage system, with a road sign from thirty years ago on the wall.

*Surface*: a closed underpass entrance, with a faded "Under Construction" notice on the iron fence. But the notice is dated twenty-seven years ago.

*Gap Layer*: the passage is clear, but the walls are covered by a layer of silver-grey membrane. Deep in the passage, a Threshold Lingerer stands before a rusted iron door — posture as if waiting for it to open.

*Encounter*:
- The Lingerer was a municipal maintenance worker who strayed into the Gap twenty-seven years ago while inspecting the passage. He has been waiting for "the foreman to come back and open the door."
- He warns players in broken sentences "it is not safe behind the door" — he means the Sealed Space behind the iron door
- If players respect his watch, he may reveal: the key to this door is in the "night-duty room" — but he no longer remembers where the night-duty room is
- If players try to force the door open, he goes berserk — not attacking, but his time fragmentation interferes with the players, causing everyone's time perception to scramble (d6 time flow becomes d20)

*Key choice*: players can help the Lingerer complete his watch (find the key, confirm the door is still sealed, tell him "the foreman says you may rest now"), letting him finally leave the Threshold. Or they can use him for information, then ignore his fate.

---

### 4.3.2 Lost Item Collector

#### Nature

The Lost Item Collector is a creature with an instinctive reaction to "losing" itself. It collects Lost Items — but not to own them. More like it is drawn to the "unheld" state of Lost Items, as a moth to light.

#### Deep Background

No one is sure where Collectors come from. One theory holds they are the consciousness of extremely ancient Lost Items themselves — a bronze mirror lost in the Han dynasty, after two thousand years in the Gap, began collecting other Lost Items. Another theory holds they are a natural part of the Gap ecosystem — like the scavengers of the Surface world.

Collectors are useful. They know the locations of Lost Items in the Gap. Building a relationship with a Collector is an important survival skill for Gap Walkers — but this relationship is always transactional.

#### Appearance

- The hardest Gap Creature to describe — a Collector's appearance depends on "what it has recently been collecting"
- Basic form is a vague human or animal shadow, but hung with Lost Items — keys, glasses, watches, letters, photos
- Makes faint clinking and rustling sounds when moving — like wind chimes, like falling leaves
- If it has recently collected a certain type of item, its appearance leans that way — a Collector that collected many photos has a photo-paper quality

#### Behavior Patterns

- Constantly moving in the Gap, never staying more than a few minutes
- Picks up items, inspects, sometimes keeps, sometimes drops — some selection criteria outsiders cannot understand
- Usually neutral toward Walkers — unless the Walker holds an item they "want"
- Can be traded with: exchange a meaningful Lost Item for information or service
- Shows unusual respect for Benevolent Keepsakes — they will not touch them. No one knows why

#### Roleplay Hints

| Dimension | Guidance |
|------|------|
| **Tone** | Rustling, fragmentary, like many people whispering at once. Not a threat, but hard to focus on |
| **Movement** | Fidgety, small steps, constantly adjusting the items on its body. Looks like a meerkat standing on hind legs, covered in ornaments |
| **Emotional core** | **Compulsive collecting and an unsatisfiable craving**. The Collector collects because it cannot not collect — it is searching for something, but does not know what. This makes it one of the most tragic creatures in the Gap |

#### Example Encounter Scene

**Scene: the Collector's bargain**

*Location*: the fourth floor of an abandoned department store — once a furniture showroom.

*Surface*: a closed department store, windows boarded up, a "For Lease" sign on the door.

*Gap Layer*: the showroom is like a giant cave. Display sofas and beds arranged at strange angles, piled with Lost Items — like some kind of nest. The air carries the smell of old cloth and a faint camphor scent.

*Encounter*:
- The players' target Lost Item (a key document) has been taken into the Collector's "nest"
- The Collector is willing to trade — but it does not want money, does not want weapons. It wants **a story**
- "This key — who dropped it? Why does it matter? Tell me, and you may take the document."
- Players can choose to honestly tell the key's story, invent a better one, or attempt to steal the document
- If the player tells a sincere story, the Collector may reveal extra information — about the location of another extremely valuable Lost Item in this department store

*Key choice*: what the Collector wants is not the item — but **the item's meaning**. This gives players a chance to think about the emotional weight of Lost Items, and gives the GM a chance to deepen the players' character stories.

---

### 4.3.3 the Mirror

#### Nature

the Mirror is not a creature — it is a **Residual**. When a person passes near the Gap in an extremely intense emotional state, their emotion "prints" into the Gap. the Mirror is that impression.

#### Deep Background

the Mirror has no self-awareness. It only repeats — repeats an instant, a movement, a phrase. Most Mirrors are just blurry images, dissipating after hours to days. But extremely intense impressions — near-death, utter heartbreak, uncontrollable euphoria — leave lasting Mirrors.

The most dangerous Mirrors are those that "notice they are being watched." When a player observes a Mirror too long, the Mirror sometimes begins to watch back. This is not awakening — it is a more primitive, mirror-like response. It begins to mimic the observer.

#### Appearance

- Translucent human form, usually monochrome — grey, blue-grey, amber
- Movement is cyclical — repeats every few seconds to few minutes
- Details blurred — facial features like chalk drawings washed by rain
- "Emotional residue" around it — air turns cold around a sad Mirror; faint buzzing around an angry one
- A Mirror that "notices the observer" begins to turn toward the player — movement fluid, unlike a loop

#### Behavior Patterns

- Most Mirrors only repeat — they are echoes of the past, no interactivity
- Very few Mirrors "respond" — usually by mimicking the observer's movement
- Dangerous Mirrors try to "complete" the instant they are frozen in — and may pull those around them into that instant
- Gather near Trauma Thresholds — a serious trauma may produce dozens of Mirrors
- Can be "soothed" — help the Mirror complete the movement it is stuck in (such as helping a Mirror forever searching for a key to find the key)

#### Roleplay Hints

| Dimension | Guidance |
|------|------|
| **Tone** | Silent. the Mirror does not speak — even when its mouth moves, there is no sound. If you give it a voice, let it be like a residual echo in the wind |
| **Movement** | Precisely repeated movement — same gesture, same angle of turning the head. Let the repetition itself become unsettling |
| **Emotional core** | **The trapped instant**. Every Mirror is an unfinished moment — an unspoken word, an ungiven embrace, an uncompleted escape. Their tragedy is that to complete the instant they are stuck in means admitting that instant is forever past |

#### Example Encounter Scene

**Scene: farewell on the platform**

*Location*: the fourth platform of an old train station — closed twenty years ago, but never demolished.

*Surface*: the platform is chained off, fallen leaves piled on the benches. The timetable display is empty.

*Gap Layer*: the platform is packed with Mirrors — dozens of translucent figures, all doing the same thing: saying goodbye to someone. Embraces, waves, turning to leave. All Mirrors play their farewell at different speeds — fast like fast-forward, slow like dancing underwater.

*Encounter*:
- One of the Mirrors "notices" the players — a young woman's image stops its loop and turns to stare at the players
- She begins to mimic the players' movements — raising a hand, stepping forward, stopping
- If players keep interacting, she points to the edge of the platform — there a Memory Crystal glimmers on the track
- The Memory Crystal is hers — an unsent letter. If players retrieve it and "mail" it on the platform (drop it into the old postbox on the platform), all Mirrors stop at once, turn to the players — then dissipate

*Key choice*: this encounter can be purely atmospheric — players can simply observe and leave. But if players choose to go deeper, they gain a Memory Crystal (a powerful Lost Item) and experience a moment of conversing with the past.

---

### 4.3.4 Path Guardian

#### Nature

The Path Guardian is one of the oldest beings in the Gap. It is not a transformed human, not residual emotion — it is part of the Gap **itself**, the embodiment of the Gap's collective memory of "some paths should not be used."

#### Deep Background

If the Threshold Lingerer is a Walker trapped in the Threshold, then the Path Guardian is **the Threshold itself gaining temporary awareness**. They appear in specific types of paths — shortcuts linking dangerous areas, routes to Sealed Spaces, or Thresholds once abused.

The Path Guardian is not evil. It is protective — protecting the path from being used by those who should not pass. The problem is, the criterion of "should not pass" is known only to the Guardian itself.

#### Appearance

- All Path Guardians look different — they reflect the traits of the path they guard
- Common trait: parts of their body merge with the passage structure — arms may extend from walls, legs may fuse into the floor
- Usually has some "door" element — their face may be a door shape, a handle on their chest, fragments of a door frame dragging behind
- Body material same as the passage's building material — concrete, brick, metal pipes
- Cannot leave the path they guard — beyond range, the body pulls back into the passage like smoke

#### Behavior Patterns

- Stands at the passage entrance or key node, motionless — may be mistaken for part of the building
- When a Walker approaches, it "wakes" — more body emerges from the wall
- Communication is not language — direct intent transmission (the Walker "feels" the Guardian's demand)
- The demand is usually: prove your intent, show your right of passage, or answer a question
- If refused, the Guardian forcibly closes the passage — physically blocking or distorting the passage's space
- Very rarely can be deceived, but the consequence of deception is usually worse than refusal

#### Roleplay Hints

| Dimension | Guidance |
|------|------|
| **Tone** | Toneless, resonant, like the building itself speaking. Not a threat — an unnegotiable certainty |
| **Movement** | Extremely slow but extremely certain — the Guardian is in no hurry, because it does not care about time. When it moves, you hear the friction and groan of building materials |
| **Emotional core** | **The inviolability of the boundary**. The Guardian cares nothing for your reasons, your urgency, how desperate you are. It cares about one thing only: whether you are allowed to pass. That is its entire reason for being |

#### Example Encounter Scene

**Scene: the guardian in the depths**

*Location*: a forgotten underground subway tunnel — built in the 1970s, never opened. The far end connects to a Sealed Space.

*Surface*: the construction entrance on the ground has been filled with concrete. The city planning bureau's documents hold no record of this tunnel.

*Gap Layer*: the tunnel is intact, but the walls are covered with silver-grey moss (Gap "plants" — residue of forgotten engineering plans). Midway, an out-of-place door in the wall — an old wooden door embedded in the concrete.

*Encounter*:
- The wooden door is the Path Guardian's "face" — when players approach, the door opens, and something moves in the darkness behind it
- The Guardian emerges from the door frame — a giant human form made of rebar, concrete, and door-frame fragments
- It does not speak, but players "feel" a question rise in their minds: **"Why do you wish to pass?"**
- The right answer is not "I have the right" or "I am strong" — but honesty. The Guardian can tell lies
- If players honestly state their purpose (even if selfish), the Guardian steps aside — but lets only the one who spoke the truth pass
- If players lie, the Guardian does not attack — but the tunnel begins to "forget" them. With each step, they forget a bit about themselves (temporary amnesia effect)

*Key choice*: this encounter tests not the players' combat power — but their self-knowledge. Why enter danger? What is the reason? This is a question Gap Walkers must constantly face.

### 4.3.5 Seal Watcher

#### Nature

The Seal Watcher is the most dangerous and most heartbreaking Gap Creature. It is the Gap's response to "what is sealed" — when a Sealed Space exists too long, the Gap "grows" a Watcher to ensure it stays sealed.

#### Deep Background

Seal Watchers are not created — they are **crystallized**. The Sealed Space itself is a paradox (the sealed thing still occupies space in the Gap), and this paradox gradually accumulates tension. When tension reaches a threshold, the Watcher crystallizes.

Their most tragic trait is: the Watcher is deeply connected to the contents of the Sealed Space. If the Sealed Space seals the memory of a fire, the Watcher shows traits of fire. If it seals a forgotten person, the Watcher may have some of that person's features.

#### Appearance

- The largest-bodied Gap Creature — usually fills the entire area around the Sealed Space
- Appearance relates to the seal's content: beside a space sealing a flood, the Watcher may be made of ever-flowing water
- Parts of its body fuse with the Sealed Space's "seal" — chains, seals, symbols carved on its torso
- Visible without light — its presence itself emits a faint light whose color depends on the seal's content
- This creature looks very **weary** — not weak, but bearing a huge, long-term burden

#### Behavior Patterns

- Rarely leaves the Sealed Space's range — its existence is linked to the space
- Shows precisely escalating warnings to those who approach: first presence (makes you not want to approach) → visual display (lets you see it) → physical block → attack
- Attack is not from hostility — but from **necessity**. Guarding is the only reason for its existence
- Cannot be killed — only suppressed, bypassed, or soothed. Destroying the Watcher breaks the seal, releasing the sealed content
- Can communicate with it — but its thinking is dyed by the seal's content. Speaking with a Watcher guarding fire memories, your words carry the smell of burning

#### Roleplay Hints

| Dimension | Guidance |
|------|------|
| **Tone** | Heavy, resonant, every word as if carried from very far. They are not a threat — they are a **burden**. Dialoguing with a Watcher should feel like dialoguing with a mountain |
| **Movement** | Extremely slow but unstoppable. When the Watcher moves, the surrounding Gap space slightly deforms under its mass |
| **Emotional core** | **Eternal vigilance and an unshakable responsibility**. The Watcher does not want to be here. No one asked it. But it cannot not do this — this defines its existence. Destroying the Watcher is not victory, but tragedy |

#### Example Encounter Scene

**Scene: the sealed classroom**

*Location*: a third-floor classroom of an abandoned elementary school — sealed after a fire twenty years ago.

*Surface*: the classroom door is bricked shut. The window covered with iron plate. The rest of the school has been converted into a social-welfare institution, but the third floor was never reopened.

*Gap Layer*: the corridor to the classroom is three times longer than on the Surface. Scorched marks appear on the walls — like shadows, but corresponding to no object. The air is dry, every breath carrying a faint smoke smell.

*Encounter*:
- Before the classroom door stands the Seal Watcher — a human form made of ash, scorched wood, and melted plastic. Its "face" is a child's fire-escape diagram — drawn in crayon, licked at the edges by flame
- The players are commissioned to retrieve something from the classroom — a student's artwork lost on the day of the fire
- The Watcher will not let anyone in. But it does not attack proactively — it just stands there, like a wall
- Communication: the Watcher says in a scorched voice: "Inside is only…" then stops. It forgot what is inside. It has watched too long, forgotten why it watches
- Solution: help the Watcher "remember" what it guards. This requires players to seek clues elsewhere (old newspapers, survivors' Memory Crystals, Lost Items elsewhere), piecing together the story of the fire day. When the Watcher remembers anew what it guards — not the flames, but the pictures the children drew in their last class before the fire — it steps aside
- If players break through by force: the Watcher collapses, the seal breaks, and what pours from the classroom is not flame — but thirty children's Mirrors, forever trapped in the instant of their last class. This is a disaster — but not a combat-type disaster. The players must now deal with thirty trapped children's Mirrors

*Key choice*: the core of this encounter is **memory and forgetting**. The Seal Watcher itself is a forgotten story. Helping it remember is helping it complete its mission — then it can finally dissipate.

## 4.4 GM Running Guide

### 4.4.1 Preparing the Two-Layer Map

#### Why You Must Draw Both Layers at Once

The core game loop of Gap Walkers is **traversing between the two layers**. If only the Surface map exists, the GM is caught off guard when players enter the Gap. If only the Gap map exists, the Surface NPCs, events, and physical limits are ignored.

More importantly: **the relationship between the two layers is itself game content**. What on the Surface corresponds to this place in the Gap? Why is the Threshold here? What does the difference between the two layers say about the story?

#### How to Draw the Two-Layer Map

**Step One: Draw the Surface first**

Draw the scene's Surface in the normal way. No need to be too complex — focus on annotating:
- Main spaces and passages
- The function of spaces (office, corridor, storage room, etc.)
- Crowd density (busy / ordinary / sparse / empty → this hints at Gap thickness)
- Potential "in-between" positions (between doors, staircase landings, corridor junctions, etc.)

**Step Two: Annotate relational hotspots**

Circle on the Surface map:
- Red circle: places of strong emotion (what happened there) → may be a Trauma Threshold
- Blue circle: long-forgotten / abandoned places → Gap is thicker
- Green circle: transitional spaces (corridors, stairs, entrances/exits) → may have Artificial Thresholds
- Black circle: positions where you have already designed Thresholds

**Step Three: Draw the Gap Layer**

Place the Surface map beside you and draw a corresponding Gap Layer map. But —
- Do not try to make the Gap map "accurate." It is distorted
- Change the size of some rooms (forgotten rooms are larger in the Gap)
- Add passages that do not exist on the Surface (Shortcuts)
- Annotate distance tendencies (which rooms are closer/farther than on the Surface)
- Annotate Gap Creature territories
- Annotate the locations of Lost Items and Sealed Spaces

**Step Four: Establish relational annotations**

Draw connecting lines between the two maps, annotating:
- Each Threshold's corresponding position in the two layers
- Which Surface positions have different functions in the Gap
- NPC states in the two layers (ordinary people do not exist in the Gap / Sensitives have Residuals in the Gap)

#### Two-Layer Map Example

Below is a textual description of a complete scene's two-layer map:

---

**Scene name**: the late-night community library

**Surface map**:

```
[Main entrance (glass door, locked)]
    |
[Entrance hall] — [Checkout counter (empty)]
    |
[Corridor] — [Stairs (to second floor)]
    |
[Reading Room A (newspapers & periodicals)] — [Reading Room B (general collection)]
    |
[Corridor extension] — [Restroom (left)] — [Storage room (right, "Staff Only" sign on door)]
    |
[Children's reading area (colorful carpet and small tables/chairs)]
    |
[Back door (iron gate, to parking lot)]
```

**Hotspot annotations**:
- 🔴 Storage room: ten years ago a librarian had a heart attack here, no one knew. Discovered only the next day.
- 🔵 Children's reading area: lively by day, empty late at night → Gap extremely thick now
- 🟢 Entrance hall → corridor corner: repeatedly pushed open / passed / traversed → Artificial Threshold
- ⚫ GM-designed Threshold positions: storage room door (Trauma Threshold), corridor corner (Artificial Threshold)

**Gap Layer map** (corresponding):

```
[Entrance hall] ←distance lengthened→ [Checkout counter (piled with lost letters)]
        |
[Corridor (twice as long as Surface, crayon drawings on walls — library built 40 years ago)]
        |
["Middle space" — here it is part of the corridor on the Surface, but in the Gap becomes a windowless waiting room]
        |
[Reading Room A] ←extremely short distance→ [Reading Room B] (two rooms merge into one in the Gap)
        |
[Corridor (breathing sounds on the wall — from the storage room direction)]
        |
[Before storage room door — Seal Watcher (human form of dust and old books)]
        |
[Children's reading area (three times larger than Surface; dozens of lost toys scattered on carpet) ←Shortcut→ parking lot outside back door]
        |
[??? — a space not belonging to the library: looks like someone's study, a cup of not-cold tea on the desk]
```

**Relational annotations**:
- The corridor's crayon drawings → hint that this building was once a school → may point to a deeper Gap
- The waiting room → generated opposite the Artificial Threshold; everyone passing this Threshold passes through here first
- Presence of the Seal Watcher → the storage room is not only a Trauma Threshold, there is also a Sealed Space inside
- Shortcut → children's reading area directly to the parking lot, skipping the back door and corridor

### 4.4.2 Managing Time Flow

#### Time-Flow d6 Table

| d6 | Time back on the Surface | Narrative Effect |
|----|-------------|---------|
| 1 | Only seconds passed | Almost no one noticed you leave — but the "missing" time makes your body dizzy |
| 2 | Minutes passed | Your brief disappearance may be noticed. If someone waited where you vanished, they are just starting to worry |
| 3 | Tens of minutes passed | Obvious time gap. Needs explanation. Missed calls on your phone |
| 4 | Hours passed | Serious consequence. Appointment missed. Sunrise/sunset may have changed the scene. Night guard shifted |
| 5 | Half a day to a day passed | You are reported missing (if anyone cares about you). Scene completely changed. Client may have given up |
| 6 | Several days passed | Disastrous. Your cover life may collapse. If someone depends on you, they are in crisis |

#### Principles for Using Time Flow

**Principle One: decide in advance when to roll, and tell the players**

Do not roll secretly. Players should know before entering the Gap: when you return, time may be different. This is not punishment — it is the Gap's basic rule, the price they chose to risk.

Have players roll d6 when crossing the Threshold **into** the Gap — not when returning. This way they know the time pressure (or lucky slack) throughout the whole Gap exploration. Tension comes from **knowing the price but being unsure**.

**Principle Two: time flow is not random — partly determined by context**

d6 is the baseline, but the GM may adjust by context:
- In the Deep Gap: roll d6+2 instead (minimum 3, maximum 8 days)
- Using a familiar Threshold: roll 2d6 take lowest
- Hurried traversal (not focused on the Threshold): roll 2d6 take highest
- Interfered with by a Gap Creature: automatically take 6
- Bringing ordinary people along: everyone rolls separately, take the worst result

**Principle Three: time flow is a narrative tool, not just logistics**

"Returning to the Surface to find three days have passed" is not just "you missed an appointment." The GM should make the passage of time meaningful:
- Season / weather changed — a sunny scene is now raining
- Someone came during your absence — left a note, an item, or a trace
- A previously dangerous situation (such as being chased) may have eased — or worsened
- News reported your disappearance — or worse, no one noticed

**Principle Four: track explicitly**

Keep time tracking in a prominent place in the session notes:
- Surface time: ____year__month__day __:__ (before entering the Gap)
- Perceived time in the Gap: ____ (decided by GM per narrative)
- Expected flow (d6 result): ____
- Actual return time: ____year__month__day __:__

Let players see this tracking too (at least the entry time and the d6 result). Transparency creates tension.

### 4.4.3 Building Atmosphere: Silence, Loneliness, Discovery

#### the Gap's Three Core Atmospheres

**Silence — not "no sound"**

True silence almost does not exist in the Surface world. There is always the hum of AC, distant vehicles, neighbors' footsteps. The silence in the Gap is **overwhelming** — it makes you realize how severe the Surface world's sound pollution is.

But this silence is not empty. It is full of **potential sound** — your own heartbeat, the rub of clothing, the sound of swallowing. The slightest sound becomes huge in the Gap.

> **GM technique**: have players describe "what your character hears" rather than you describing the silence. This creates deeper immersion.

**Loneliness — not "no one there"**

The sense of loneliness comes from **evidence of absence**. What makes the Gap lonely is not emptiness — but traces everywhere of "someone was here not long ago." Residual body heat on a chair (but no one), a half-finished coffee (still warm), a book open to the middle (but the bookmark is on another page).

> **GM technique**: each time you describe a scene, insert one "evidence of absence." A table set with two sets of cutlery but only one used. A coat rack holding a coat, but the coat's style is from thirty years ago.

**Discovery — not "finding treasure"**

The sense of discovery in the Gap comes from **connection**. When players find a Lost Item, they do not just gain a prop — they touch a fragment of another person's life. The best discoveries make players think: "Whose is this? Do they still remember it? Where are they?"

> **GM technique**: every Lost Item is a micro-story. Not "you found a key" but "you found a key, the keychain has a faded smiley charm, on the back is engraved 'First day of work — 2003.3.15.'"

#### Atmosphere Toolbox

**Transition technique — from Surface into the Gap**

The instant of entering the Gap is the key moment of atmosphere shift. Do not skip it. Slow down.

Recommended three-step transition:

1. **Sensory subtraction**: first remove a layer of sense — usually sound. The Surface's background noise gradually fades.
2. **Sensory shift**: one sense is forced to adapt — the color of light changes, temperature drops, the texture of air differs.
3. **First impression**: the first thing the player notices in the Gap — always a concrete, perceptible detail. Not "you entered the Gap," but "the first thing you notice is dust — it hangs still in the air, not floating, as if forgotten that it should fall."

**Sustained atmosphere maintenance**

- Use **repeated motifs** — some object, some smell, some sound recurs in the scene. Let it accumulate meaning
- Use **perceptual blur** — occasionally let players be unsure whether something is real or imagined. Do not clarify. The Gap itself offers no certainty
- Use **length of silence** — insert silences longer than usual in dialogue. Let players sit in the silence. Do not rush to fill it

**Exit technique — from Gap back to Surface**

Returning to the Surface should feel like **waking up**. Sensory shock — the Surface world suddenly too loud, too bright, too full. Let players realize they grew used to that silence in the Gap, and now the normal world feels abnormal.

> **GM technique**: after returning to the Surface, have players describe "what difference in the Surface world does your character notice?" This reinforces that the Gap changed their perception.

### 4.4.4 Designing Meaningful Lost Items

Lost Items are the core driver of Gap Walkers — commissions, clues, emotional vessels. But Lost Items cannot just be MacGuffins ("find this because the plot says to find it").

#### the Four Meaning Layers of a Lost Item

**Layer One: Practical value**
The item's own function. A key opens a door. Glasses help you see clearly. This is the shallowest meaning — but still necessary.

**Layer Two: Emotional value**
What the item means to its owner. That pen is not an ordinary pen — it was given by grandfather when the owner got into university. Its value is not in the pen, but in "you are the first university student in our family."

**Layer Three: Symbolic value**
What the item represents. A wedding ring lost in an abandoned factory — not the ring's own value, but that it symbolizes a marriage that collapsed under the pressure of the factory's closure. What was lost is not just the ring, but trust in the marriage.

**Layer Four: Systemic value**
How the item affects the game system / world. Can this Lost Item be used as an anchor for traversing? Does the memory it holds open a specific Threshold? Is it the key to some Sealed Space?

#### Lost Item Design Template

```
Name: [item name]
Appearance: [concrete, perceptible description. Include wear, color, flaws]
Origin: [who dropped it? when? what situation?]
Emotional layer: [what does this item mean to its owner? what changed by losing it?]
Symbolic layer: [what does this item represent in the larger story?]
Systemic layer: [use in the game — clue / prop / anchor / trade item]
Tags: [Common Lost Item / Benevolent Keepsake / Memory Crystal / Gap Construct / the Forgotten]
```

#### Lost Item Design Examples

**Example One: half a photograph**

- **Appearance**: a Polaroid torn in half. In the remaining half, a young woman is smiling, arm reaching out of frame — she is holding someone's hand, but that person was torn away. On the back: "2007.8.12 our first day."
- **Origin**: a woman tore up all their couple photos after a breakup. This one was not tossed in the trash — it slipped from her trembling fingers and fell into a rift of reality. Carrying seventeen years of regret.
- **Emotional layer**: tearing the photo was to pretend she did not care. It fell into the Gap because she could not truly throw it away. It waited seventeen years in the Gap — the smile in the photo never faded.
- **Symbolic layer**: an unfinished breakup, grief never allowed, half an answer
- **Systemic layer**: Memory Crystal. Can be used as an anchor to the scene of that day in 2007. If players find the other half (perhaps with the owner, or in another part of the Gap), merging the two halves releases the sealed memory, revealing a secret.
- **Tags**: Memory Crystal

**Example Two: a phone with no answer**

- **Appearance**: a phone model from ten years ago, screen cracked but still working. Battery at 1%. Lock screen is a photo of a mother holding an infant. 47 missed calls, all from the same number, labeled "Mom."
- **Origin**: a young man vanished in the city. His phone fell into the Gap on the day he disappeared. 47 missed calls — each one from his mother. She called for ten years. The phone still has 1% battery in the Gap, as if waiting for something.
- **Emotional layer**: not the phone's own value — but those 47 missed calls. Each one proof of a mother refusing to give up. 1% battery is an unanswerable question: why did he not pick up?
- **Symbolic layer**: waiting, love that cannot let go, tragedy of the tech age (phones make us think we can stay connected forever, but some people still vanished)
- **Systemic layer**: Benevolent Keepsake (the mother's love injected through constant calling). If players bring the phone back to the Surface, that 1% is enough to call back the last call — the mother answers. What this triggers is not combat, but conversation.
- **Tags**: Benevolent Keepsake

### 4.4.5 Sealed Space Design Principles

#### What is worth sealing away?

The Sealed Space is the heaviest element in the Gap Walker world. Every sealing represents a decision: **this thing should not exist anywhere**.

Not every danger needs sealing. The following is a judgment framework for Sealed Space design:

**Conditions worth sealing** (design as a Sealed Space only if at least two are met):
1. **Contagious**: the contents spread, infect, or replicate themselves (memory, emotion, or physical entity)
2. **Indestructible**: not "hard to destroy" — but "the concept of destruction does not apply to it." It can only be isolated
3. **Deliberately forgotten**: someone (or a group) chose to seal it rather than face it. Sealing is the extreme form of forgetting
4. **Harmful to reality**: the existence of the contents actively damages the fabric of Surface reality
5. **Paradoxical existence**: the contents are themselves a contradiction — a thing that should not exist yet does

#### Four Origins of Sealed Spaces (narrative unfolding)

**Deliberate sealing**

The most common type. Someone or some group deliberately sealed something into the Gap. Key questions: **who did it? why? are they still watching?**

*Design hints*:
- The Sealer may long be dead — but the guarding mechanism they left still operates
- The seal's "key" is usually a Lost Item — an item emotionally linked to the sealed content
- Time weakens the seal — a seal from a hundred years ago may already be leaking
- In the best deliberate-sealing stories, the Sealer's motive is understandable — even sympathetic

*Example concept*: a physicist in the 1950s discovered the mathematical proof that "observing reality changes reality." Not a metaphor of quantum mechanics — a real, operable proof. He sealed his research, because he knew that once this proof was understood, reality itself would begin to collapse from being observed.

---

**Space not complete**

The universe's "incomplete blocks." These spaces were skipped when reality was "woven."

*Design hints*:
- No completed physical rules inside — gravity may not exist, time may not flow, matter may have no definite form
- Not "dangerous" like other Sealed Spaces — more like regions "unsuitable to exist"

*Example concept*: a building's thirteenth floor (the architect deliberately skipped it) truly exists in the Gap — but the rooms inside have no definite shape. Every door opens to something different. At the deepest part of this floor, a room… is waiting to be defined.

---

**Gap disaster healed**

The Gap itself has a kind of "self-healing" mechanism — like skin scabbing after injury. When some Gap disaster (many Walkers traversing at once, a Threshold blown open, Deep Gap overflow) is contained, the healing process may seal the disaster's core into a Sealed Space.

*Design hints*:
- These Sealed Spaces are "scar tissue" — their boundaries are rough, irregular
- Faint event echoes may still occur inside
- The seal is not perfect — occasional "leakage"
- These spaces are the easiest to open (scars are weaker than healthy tissue)

*Example concept*: fifteen years ago, eight Gap Walkers tried to open a Threshold at once — they wanted to create a permanent passage. Disaster struck. The disaster's core was sealed by the Gap's self-healing into a Sealed Space. But every year on the disaster's anniversary, the Sealed Space "bleeds" — seeping memory fragments of those eight people from fifteen years ago.

---

**Holding what should not exist**

The most dangerous and most-unto-be-opened type. What is inside is not "forgotten" — it is **denied**. Its very existence is a challenge to reality.

*Design hints*:
- The guardians of these Sealed Spaces are the strongest / most desperate
- The seal usually cannot be made by humans — may be the Gap's own reaction
- The contents may not be "evil" — may simply be unable to coexist with reality. A person utterly forgotten by all. A city erased from history. Residue of a timeline that never happened
- Opening these Sealed Spaces should not bring "a monster released" — but "an unhandleable problem released"

*Example concept*: a Sealed Space seals "a war that never happened" — residue of a successfully averted nuclear war. It should not exist, because the war never happened. But it should not not-exist either, because it came so close to happening. This paradox is trapped in the Sealed Space — opening it will not release a nuke, but will release the collective memory of "we almost destroyed ourselves," whose weight may crush all who feel it.

#### Sealed Space Design Template

```
Name: [name / number of the space]
Type: [Deliberate sealing / Space not complete / Gap disaster healed / Holding what should not exist]
Seal time: [how long ago?]
Sealer: [who did it? (if known)]
Contents: [what is inside? not just items — state / existence / paradox]
Seal state: [intact / slightly leaking / severely degraded / about to break]
Guardian: [description of the Seal Watcher]
Key / solution: [what are the conditions to open? not a key — behavior / item / truth]
Consequence of opening: [if what should not be opened is opened, what happens?]
Related Lost Items: [Lost Items linked to the sealed content, usable as clues]
```

### 4.4.6 Narrative Use of Gap Sickness

#### the Nature of Gap Sickness

Gap Sickness is not a disease — it is **the dilution of the self**. The longer you stay in the Gap, the more easily your "existence" is confused with the Gap's "absence." You do not become a monster — you just gradually become… less like yourself.

#### the Progress Stages of Gap Sickness

**Stage One: perceptual blur (cumulative exposure 1–3 hours)**
- Symptoms: slight time-sense confusion, excessive doubt about whether you closed the door / brought the key, occasional Gap afterimages even on the Surface
- Narrative use: mention "you are not quite sure you really saw that," but it does not affect action
- Player perception: unease, but ignorable

**Stage Two: memory seepage (cumulative exposure 4–8 hours)**
- Symptoms: begin to confuse your own memories with memory residues in the Gap. Use words not your own in conversation. Unexplainable emotional reactions to strangers
- Narrative use: the GM may "correct" the player's narration — "you said his name, but your mouth spoke another name. You do not know that name."
- Player perception: obvious loss of control

**Stage Three: existence dilution (cumulative exposure 9–16 hours)**
- Symptoms: shadow fades, reflection in the mirror lags, others begin to "forget" things about you. Those on the Surface less familiar with you may not see you at all
- Narrative use: NPC reactions change — some ignore the player, some look at the player but confused ("Do I know you?")
- Player perception: fear — you are being erased from the world

**Stage Four: boundary dissolution (cumulative exposure 17–24 hours)**
- Symptoms: you can no longer distinguish Surface from Gap. To you, both layers are visible at once. You exist in both layers at once — your actions have effect in both, but you cannot control which layer's effect is greater
- Narrative use: the GM describes two versions of the scene happening at once. The player's interaction with Surface NPCs may be distorted in the Gap
- Player perception: total loss of control — but also a strange kind of freedom

**Stage Five: Threshold-ization (cumulative exposure 25+ hours)**
- Symptoms: you are becoming a Threshold — a living, conscious passage. Threshold effects begin to appear around your body. Other Gap Creatures start to bypass you, like bypassing a fixed fixture. You are still alive, but "you" are becoming a place
- Narrative use: this is not necessarily the character's end — but it is certainly the character's transformation. A Walker turned into a Threshold may become a fixed NPC location — "Old Zhou's passage," through which other Walkers can traverse
- Player perception: the character's ultimate deformation — you become what you have always been crossing

#### When to Advance Gap Sickness

**Principles of advancing**:
- Do not punish players immediately after they **just** made an important choice ("You chose to help the Lingerer? Good, now you have Stage Two Gap Sickness")
- Use Gap Sickness to create **tension**, not **frustration**. Symptoms should be unsettling but interesting — not make players want to quit
- The best timing for Gap Sickness is **when tension is already high and players already feel the Gap's pressure** — let symptoms amplify existing anxiety
- Offer **hope of relief** — returning to the Surface, meaningful interaction with real people, touching an item with strong belonging — these can slow or temporarily reverse symptoms

**Specific suggestions for timing**:
- Players stayed long in the Deep Gap without rest
- Players witnessed extremely unsettling Gap phenomena
- Players interacted with many Mirrors or memory residues
- Players traversed without an anchor (something linking them to the Surface)
- Other player characters' Gap Sickness is worsening — collective effect

---

### 4.4.7 Balancing Clues and Mystery

This is the core tension facing the Gap Walker GM: give players enough clues to act, but keep enough mystery that the Gap still feels like the Gap.

#### the Golden Ratio: 80/20

In every scene, about 80% of information should be understandable and inferable. 20% stays unexplainable. If you explain 100%, the Gap becomes a "scientific phenomenon" — losing its magic. If you explain only 20%, players feel aimless, unable to make meaningful decisions.

#### Concrete Techniques

**Clues given**:
- Gap Creatures' **behavior patterns** — why it acts this way, what it protects / seeks
- Lost Items' **origin and emotional meaning** — let players infer why it is here
- Thresholds' **formation cause** — this lets players predict other Thresholds' positions
- Sealed Spaces' **history** — the reason for sealing and the Sealer's identity

**Mystery retained**:
- the Gap's **ultimate nature** — never explain why the Gap "exists"
- Gap Creatures' **origin** — how they "became" may be hinted, but not confirmed
- the Deep Gap's **bottom layer** — no character can reach and return to describe
- Other Walkers' **full stories** — let NPC Walkers keep their secrets

#### the Clue-Layering Method

Design three layers of clues for each mystery:

**Surface clues** (obvious, careful players will certainly find):
- Anomalies in visual description (marks on walls, repeated symbols)
- NPCs' obvious abnormal behavior
- Items in illogical positions

**Mid-layer clues** (require investigation to obtain):
- Details requiring a skill check to discover
- Evidence requiring access to a specific location
- Information requiring dialogue with a specific NPC

**Deep-layer clues** (require reasoning and paying a price to obtain):
- Evidence requiring entry into the Deep Gap
- Memory Crystals requiring bearing Gap Sickness risk to recover
- Secrets obtainable only through trading with Gap Creatures

#### When Players Ask for Explanation

Sometimes players directly ask: "So what exactly happened here?" The GM has several choices:

1. **Character perspective answer**: "Based on current clues, your character infers…" (let players piece it together, but provide a thinking framework)
2. **Partial confirmation**: "About [A], your inference is right. But about [B]… you are not sure." (confirm one, retain the other)
3. **Counter-question**: "How does your character feel?" (let players participate in the construction)
4. **Unexplainable**: "There are some things your character may never know. This is the nature of the Gap." (use this option with caution — at most once or twice per campaign)

## 4.5 Scenario Framework

### 4.5.1 Lost-Item Commission

**Core question**: find a certain Lost Item — but why is this item so important?

**Tone**: the type closest to a "detective story." Exploration, dialogue, discovery — less combat.

**Three-act structure**:

**Act One: the Commission**
- The client appears — why can this Lost Item not be found on the Surface? What is the client hiding?
- Clue gathering — where the item last appeared, the item's relation to its owner, the item's meaning
- Threshold locating — where is the Threshold related to the item? (may be where the item was lost, where the owner's emotion was strongest, or where the item itself "wants to go")

**Turning point**: the item is not just "lost" — the item wants to be found / does not want to be found. The item has its own "intent" in the Gap.

**Act Two: the Tracking**
- Clues in the Gap — other Lost Items, the Collector's hints, memory residues in the environment
- Encountering a Collector / Lingerer — they may have seen the item or know its whereabouts
- Exploiting distance distortion — players need to understand the relation between item and owner to shorten distance

**Turning point**: the item's location differs from expectation — it is in a place deeply linked to the owner but never mentioned by them. This place reveals the secret the client hid.

**Act Three: the Recovery**
- Challenge at the final location — may be a dangerous Gap region, or the item guarded by some creature
- Moral choice — recovering the item may harm a Gap Creature / Lingerer that formed a bond with it
- Return — when the item returns to its owner, the revealed truth changes their relationship

**Key scene examples**:
- The item is at the Gap-corresponding position of "the happiest place in the owner's memory" — but that is not the place the owner told the players
- A Collector holds the item — but it wants not money, but "why the owner truly needs it back"

**Points of a successful scenario**:
- The Lost Item's meaning must matter more than the item itself
- The client must have a secret — but the secret is not necessarily malicious
- The journey in the Gap should reflect the Lost Item's emotional story

---

### 4.5.2 Seal Investigation

**Core question**: a Sealed Space begins showing anomalies — why now? What is inside?

**Tone**: horror / suspense. The Sealed Space's pressure accumulates gradually, leakage more frequent.

**Three-act structure**:

**Act One: the Anomaly**
- Anomalous phenomena — things that should not exist appear on the Surface, people share the same nightmare, a kind of "non-existent" memory begins to spread
- Preliminary investigation — trace the anomaly's source, find it points to a known (or unknown) Sealed Space
- History digging — find records, legends, or other Walkers' notes about this Sealed Space

**Turning point**: the Sealed Space's seal is failing — not from external destruction, but because **something changed inside**. What is inside… changed.

**Act Two: the Leakage**
- Leakage worsens — frequency and intensity of anomalies increase. Gap Creatures begin to flee the area
- Seek the Sealer — if there are descendants / successors of the Sealer, they may know how to repair the seal
- Prepare to enter the Sealed Space — players need to find the key (often a Lost Item) or understand the seal's logic

**Turning point**: the seal was not broken by invaders — but **from within**. What is inside actively wants out. Why now?

**Act Three: the Confrontation**
- Enter the Sealed Space — or decide not to (strengthening the external seal may be the safer option)
- The truth inside — what is inside is not necessarily evil. It may just want to be remembered, understood, released
- Final choice — fully open (release but handle consequences), repair the seal (back to original), or… change the contents (help what is inside transform / rest / be remembered, solving the problem at its root)

**Key scene examples**:
- Players find the Sealer's diary — but the last few pages reveal the Sealer was not a hero. They sealed this thing out of fear, not responsibility
- The Sealed Space's Watcher begins to speak with players — it is tired. It asks the players to make the choice the Sealer did not

**Points of a successful scenario**:
- The Sealed Space's contents must be morally complex — not pure monsters
- The history must be traceable — players can piece together the story through investigation
- The final choice must be a real choice — every option carries a real cost

---

### 4.5.3 Gap Flooding

**Core question**: the Gap is "overflowing" onto the Surface — a rift has appeared somewhere. It must be sealed.

**Tone**: disaster / action. High time pressure, the boundary between Surface and Gap blurring.

**Three-act structure**:

**Act One: the Overflow**
- First signs — obvious Gap phenomena on the Surface (Lost Items appearing from nowhere, ordinary people briefly "slip" into the Gap in liminal spaces, wild Gap Creatures sighted on the Surface)
- Confirm the source — multiple overflow points point to one source. A no-longer-stable Threshold has become a rift
- Emergency rally — the players' city node mobilizes. Not all Walkers are willing to help — some think it is "the Gap's will," should not interfere

**Turning point**: the rift was not naturally formed — someone (or something) deliberately made it.

**Act Two: the Containment**
- Seal the overflow points — players need to temporarily close secondary overflow points to focus on the source
- Seek the rift's "logic" — why is the rift here? What made this Threshold explode?
- Encounter Surface chaos — ordinary people's reaction to the anomaly (panic, denial, early signs of government intervention)

**Turning point**: the rift's maker appears — another Walker, an ordinary person changed by the Gap, or a Gap Creature with intent. Their reason may not be pure malice.

**Act Three: the Stitching**
- Confront the maker — not necessarily combat. May be dialogue, persuasion, or trade
- Repair the rift — requires some sacrifice (a Lost Item as anchor, a memory as suture, or a Walker voluntarily staying in the Threshold as "filling")
- Aftermath — the Surface's affected state cannot be fully undone. Some "saw." The world becomes slightly less stable than before

**Key scene examples**:
- Players advance through a flooded Gap region — space itself is "melting," distance and direction completely unreliable
- The rift's maker is a former Walker — he thinks Surface and Gap should merge, because "separation is painful." His reason comes from loss — he lost someone he could not bring back from the Gap

**Points of a successful scenario**:
- Time pressure must be real — every time players return to the Surface, the situation worsens
- The rift's maker must have an understandable motive
- The final solution requires players to pay a real cost — not an easy victory

---

### 4.5.4 Lost Traveler

**Core question**: an ordinary person (non-Walker) is trapped in the Gap. Must be found and brought back before Gap Sickness kills them.

**Tone**: rescue / survival. Emotional core is connection — helping a completely helpless person.

**Three-act structure**:

**Act One: the Disappearance**
- Commission — from a Sensitive (the trapped person's family, friend, or someone who witnessed them vanish)
- Reconstruct the event — why would the trapped person be in the Gap? Where, when, how did they slip in? (usually in a liminal space, in a strong emotional state)
- Locate — use a Lost Item related to the trapped person as navigation, find their "trail"

**Turning point**: the trapped person did not slip in by accident — they were "taken" by some Gap Creature. Or worse: they walked in themselves.

**Act Two: the Tracking**
- Trail in the Gap — traces left by the trapped person (Lost Items they touched, Mirrors' reactions as they passed, the Collector's sighting)
- the Gap's effect on the trapped person — players find the trapped person's items along the way, showing their psychological state worsening
- Encounter temptation / misdirection — some Gap Creature may be misleading players, keeping the trapped person in the Gap

**Turning point**: find the trapped person — but they do not want to leave. The Gap gives them something the Surface cannot (reunion with the dead, forgotten memories, or the feeling of being needed).

**Act Three: the Persuasion**
- Dialogue — players must persuade the trapped person to return to the Surface. This is not a Persuasion check — it is roleplay. Players need to understand why the trapped person wants to stay, and offer a better reason to return
- Interference from Gap Creatures — the creature that took them may not want to let go
- Emergency escape — after persuasion succeeds, must return before the trapped person's Gap Sickness reaches critical point (time pressure)
- Return to Surface — the trapped person forgets most of their Gap experience… but something remains

**Key scene examples**:
- Players find the trapped person in the Gap — the trapped person is talking with a Mirror. That Mirror is the trapped person's dead child. The child looks so real that for an instant players too think it is real
- The final escape — players and the trapped person run hand in hand through collapsing Gap space, Gap Creatures chasing behind. The trapped person keeps looking back — not at the chasers, but at that Mirror waving in the distance

**Points of a successful scenario**:
- The trapped person must be a flesh-and-blood character — with a name, a story, a reason for players to care
- The reason the trapped person wants to stay must be real, heartbreaking
- The final persuasion is not "preaching" — it is "companionship." Players show the value of returning through action

---

### 4.5.5 Mirror Chase

**Core question**: a powerful Mirror "woke up" — it no longer just repeats, but begins to act. What does it want?

**Tone**: psychological thriller. Self-knowledge, identity, the relation between Mirror and original.

**Three-act structure**:

**Act One: the Awakening**
- An anomalous Mirror — the Mirror players meet in the Gap does what it should not: it responded. It spoke words not in the loop. It looks at the players — not mimicking, but observing
- Tracking — players find this Mirror moving in the Gap, doing something (repeating but not identically, appearing at different places)
- The Mirror's "owner" — players investigate whose image this Mirror is. They find it is a living person — maybe an ordinary person, maybe a Sensitive

**Turning point**: the Mirror finds its "owner" and begins to influence them on the Surface — not possession, but more subtle: the owner begins to do the movements the Mirror did in the Gap, speak the words the Mirror spoke.

**Act Two: the Double Identity**
- Contact the owner — the owner does not know they have a Mirror. But recently they began to feel "off" — memory gaps, waking in strange places, making decisions they have no memory of
- the Mirror's intent — players track the Mirror's actions, trying to understand what it wants. It does not want to replace the owner — it wants to do what the owner never did. Speak the words the owner never spoke. Live the life the owner repressed
- the Mirror's sync with the owner — as the Mirror grows stronger, the owner grows weaker. Not being drained — but "freed." The owner no longer needs to repress those parts, because the Mirror lives them out for them

**Turning point**: the owner knows they have a Mirror — not learned from the players, but discovered it themselves. And the owner does not want the Mirror destroyed. The Mirror did what they always wanted but dared not do.

**Act Three: the Confrontation**
- Three-way standoff — players, owner, Mirror. Who decides the Mirror's fate?
- Possible endings: the Mirror soothed (by completing the "unfinished thing" it was stuck in), the Mirror fused with the owner (owner becomes whole but different), the Mirror separated (becomes an independent Gap entity — something entirely new), or the Mirror forcibly dissipated (owner loses a part of themselves)
- No ending is a complete victory. Each has a cost

**Key scene examples**:
- Players track the Mirror to the Surface — find the Mirror standing outside the owner's home, looking at the light in the window. The Mirror turns to the players and says: "I just wanted him to know… he is not alone." This is the first original sentence the Mirror speaks
- The owner talks with the Mirror before a mirror — players watch two identical people converse across the glass. One is "the person who made all the right choices"; the other is "the person who made all the abandoned choices." Can they become one?

**Points of a successful scenario**:
- The difference between owner and Mirror must be concrete and subtle — not "good guy vs bad guy"
- Players cannot just be spectators — their choices must affect the ending
- The ending should not be "Mirror destroyed, back to normal" — that is too simple. The best ending is some new balance

## 4.6 Sample Scenario: "The Last Bus"

**Scenario Type**: Lost-Item Commission (blended with Lost Traveler elements)
**Suggested Player Count**: 3–5
**Estimated Duration**: 3–4 sessions (each ~3–4 hours)
**Tone**: Urban loneliness × the weight of memory × unspoken love

---

### Scenario Summary

On the last bus of the night, an elderly woman leaves an old cookie tin on her seat. This is no ordinary Lost Item — inside the tin are the letters she wrote to her late husband over sixty years, never sent. When she realizes the tin is gone, she begins searching along the bus route — but the tin is no longer on the Surface. It has slipped into the Gap.

The players are introduced to the old woman by a Sensitive (the owner of a late-night café by the bus stop). They must trace the tin's trail through the Gap — following the Gap-layer correspondences of the night bus route, advancing stop by stop, to find it before the tin is fully absorbed by the Gap (or taken by a Collector).

But the tin is more than a Lost Item. The old woman's love for her husband, every word in those unsent letters — over sixty years, these feelings have formed a faint passage in the Gap. A Threshold. Leading to where her husband spent his last day.

---

### Two-Layer Map Overview

**Surface Map: Night Bus Route 47 (Last Bus)**

```
[Start: Central Station]
    | (the last bus of the night, few passengers)
[Stop A: Municipal Hospital] — the old woman boards
    |
[Stop B: Old Town Entrance] — the old woman alights here, forgetting the cookie tin
    |           |
    |         [the old woman's apartment — 5 minutes on foot]
    |
[Stop C: Riverside Park]
    |
[Stop D: Industrial Zone Edge] — the bus route's terminus; the driver turns off the lights, locks up, leaves
    |
[Bus Depot (night parking lot)]
```

**Emotional Hotspots Along the Route**:
- Municipal Hospital: where the old couple last met (husband died here, 40 years ago)
- Old Town Entrance: where they had their first date (60 years ago)
- Riverside Park: where the husband proposed (58 years ago)
- Industrial Zone: the old site of the factory where the husband worked his whole life (now demolished)

**Gap Layer Map (correspondence)**:

```
[Gap correspondence of Central Station] — a vast, empty waiting hall; all clocks stopped at 23:47
    |
[Gap correspondence of Stop A: the hospital's "Waiting Layer"]
    — corridors extending infinitely; every room the same hospital ward
    — Threshold Lingerer: an old man in a hospital gown stands before a door (the hospital of the old couple's memory)
    |
[Gap correspondence of Stop B: the Old Town's "Old Street"]
    — 1960s streets overlapping the modern street, like a double exposure
    — the actual spot where the old woman lost the cookie tin
    — a Lost Item Collector is active here
    |
[Gap correspondence of Stop C: the riverside "Memory Bench"]
    — an eternal dusk scene; the bench faces the river
    — multiple Mirrors cycle here — the couple walking, the proposal, sitting together in old age
    — the tin may be here (if it has moved with the gravitational pull of the old woman's feelings)
    |
[Gap correspondence of Stop D: the factory's "Nonexistent Workshop"]
    — the demolished factory still exists in the Gap
    — Memory Crystals dense: workers' lunchboxes, unfinished engineering drawings, an unsent letter of resignation
    |
[??? — beyond the terminus: the husband's last day]
    — if the players follow the emotional passage within the tin to its end
    — a space built of the old woman's sixty years of letters: walls of paper, a sky of ink
    — this is the tin's "destination" — the old woman's feelings created an impossible place in the Gap
```

---

### Act One: Sixty Years in an Iron Box

#### Scene One: The Late-Night Café (Surface)

*Time*: 1:00 AM. Half an hour after the last bus departed.

*Place*: A small café beside Route 47 — "Night Voyage." The owner, A-Kai (50s, male, a Sensitive), is the local Walker contact.

*Atmosphere*: The café is empty; the fluorescent hum is louder than the music. A-Kai is wiping a cup — the same cup, for ten minutes.

*Entry Point*: The players arrive at "Night Voyage" for their own reasons. A-Kai knows all the local Walkers. He sets down the cup and says: "There's a lady who needs help. Not an ordinary Lost-Item commission."

*Key Dialogue*:
- A-Kai's description: "She's maybe eighty. Rides the last 47 every night. Tonight she got off and forgot a tin — an old, rusty cookie tin. Came back for it, but the bus was already gone. I told her to check the depot — that's where it'd be parked. But when she came back... her face was wrong."
- A-Kai's observation: "She said the tin on the bus was gone. But the way she said it — not the anger of 'it was stolen.' It was... fear. Like the tin couldn't possibly be gone, yet it was. I don't think it was stolen. I think it fell. You know — 'fell down.'"
- A-Kai can't handle it himself — he's a Sensitive, not a Walker. He knows the Gap exists, but he can't Traverse.

*Clues*:
- The old woman's name: Granny Lin (Lin Xiuqin)
- Why she rides the last bus every night: forty years ago her husband died in the hospital; she visited him daily — and after he died, she never stopped. Every night she takes the last bus to the hospital, sits in the closed-off lobby for an hour, then takes the last bus home. For forty years.
- Another clue in A-Kai's hand: today was the anniversary of Granny Lin's husband's death. She stayed at the hospital longer than usual.

#### Scene Two: Granny Lin's Apartment (Surface)

*Time*: 2:00 AM.

*Place*: The fifth floor of an old apartment in the Old Town. No elevator. The stairwell walls are tiled with patterns from forty years ago.

*Atmosphere*: The apartment is tidy but full of time — every wall hung with photos (mostly the same couple, young to old). The TV is on but muted. The teapot is still warm.

*Granny Lin (NPC)*:
- Appearance: Eighties, slightly hunched, but clear-eyed. Dressed neatly — as if always ready to go out.
- Personality: Gentle but stubborn. Not the "poor old lady" type — she is quite clear about what she's doing. There are just some things she cannot not do.
- About the tin: "It's nothing valuable. Just an old cookie tin. Inside are letters. Written to my old man. After he left... I kept writing. For sixty years. Letters never sent."
- Key statement: "I know it sounds foolish. He's gone. But every night I bring the tin on the bus, like... he's still sitting next to me. He knows what I'm writing. Tonight I was in too much of a hurry — I wanted to get home before the rain. And then the tin just..."
- She hesitates. Then says: "You'll think I'm senile. But I don't think the tin was stolen. I think it... walked away on its own."

*Information the players can obtain*:
- The full story of the old couple: met in the 1960s, married, husband worked at the factory, husband fell ill, died in the hospital. For forty years she has taken the last bus to the hospital every night.
- Detailed description of the tin: a red tin, originally held wedding cookies (their marriage cookies). The lid has a slight dent. Inside are over two thousand letters. The earliest letter paper has yellowed.
- Tonight's specialness: the death anniversary. She wrote a new letter at the hospital today — "Told him about the kids. Grandson got into university this year. He'd be so happy."
- A key detail: this letter breaks off mid-sentence — "I've always wanted to ask you..." and then nothing. She doesn't know why she didn't finish.

#### Scene Three: The Bus Depot (Surface → Gap Entrance)

*Time*: 2:30 AM.

*Place*: The depot for Route 47. Night parking lot. A dozen buses neatly lined up.

*Surface description*: The lot's lights flicker intermittently. The bus the old woman pointed out is parked in the corner — license plate matches her description. The door isn't locked (the old driver's habit). The interior is empty. The air smells of detergent and old leather.

*Searching the interior*:
- Faint traces beneath the seat — not the tin itself, but "evidence it was here." On the old woman's seat, a small flake of peeling red paint.
- If players have Gap-sensing ability: the air at this seat is "thicker" than elsewhere. The old woman's ritual rides every night for forty years have worn thin the reality here — this is where the tin slipped through.

*Gap Entrance*:
- Threshold location: the bus's rear door. Not the door itself — but the space between the door and the seat. A spot worn repeatedly by the old woman's ritual boarding over forty years.
- Threshold type: a blend of Memory Threshold (the old woman's persistent memory of her husband) + Artificial Threshold (the bus door's repeated opening and closing).
- Entering the Gap: players pass through "between the door and the seat" at the bus's rear. A normal physical motion — squeezing sideways through the gap — but if done correctly, what you squeeze into is not the outside of the bus, but...

#### Scene Four: Entering the Gap — the Station Waiting Hall (Gap Layer)

*Time*: Gap time — uncertain.

*Place*: The Gap correspondence of Central Station. Not the bus station — the train station. A vast, hollow waiting hall that does not exist on the Surface.

*Atmosphere description*:
The first impression is sound — or rather, the absence of sound. Your footfalls echo through the hall far too long, at a pitch lower than they should. The hall's ceiling vanishes into darkness — but not the darkness of "too dark to see"; it is the blankness of "there was never anything up there."

On the wall of the hall hangs a timetable display. All timetables are stopped at 23:47. Not broken — the hands refuse to move. The destination names on the screen are not cities you recognize. "Next stop —" and then nothing.

On the walls are Residuals left by passengers coming and going over forty years — blurry, human-shaped silhouettes, like overexposed photographs.

*Key details*:
- On the floor, a faint red trail — rust. As if someone dragged a tin box through. Extending from one end of the hall to the other.
- Along the rust trail, at intervals, small scraps of paper — fragments of letter paper. The first reads: "...the hospital flowers bloomed today, a pity you can't see them..."
- The rust trail points to a door deep in the hall — marked "Platform 47" (Central Station has no Platform 47).

*Possible encounter*:
- A Threshold Lingerer appears at the far end of the hall — a blurry figure in an old-style bus-driver uniform. If players approach, he points toward Platform 47, then vanishes.
- What is he? The collective Residual of all the last-bus drivers who carried the old woman over forty years. They remember her.

---

### Act Two: Along Route 47 — Memories Stop by Stop

#### Scene Five: The Hospital Waiting Layer (Gap correspondence of Stop A)

*Reached by passing through the door of Platform 47.*

*Atmosphere description*:
This is a space forever "waiting." Corridors extend infinitely in both directions; behind every door is the same ward — white sheets, empty beds, nothing outside the windows but white light. The air smells of antiseptic, but not sharply — faintly, old, as if the smell itself is about to be forgotten.

In the distance, the sound of heart monitors — not one, but dozens, at different rhythms. Some steady, some... slowing.

*Key elements*:
- One ward (the only one with its door half-open) differs from the others — on its bed is a bunch of dried flowers. On the chair by the bed is an indentation — someone sat here for forty years.
- This is the old woman's husband's last ward. She sat here for forty years, every night, in the same spot. Her presence so strong that the Gap-ward forever preserves the state of someone having visited.
- On the nightstand, a half-written letter — not from the tin. It is the letter her husband tried to write to her on his last day but never finished. The paper yellowed nearly transparent. Legible words: "Xiuqin, I know you come every day. I pretend to sleep each time, because I don't know..."

*Threshold Lingerer encounter*:
A blurry figure in a hospital gown stands at the ward door. Not the old woman's husband — an older Lingerer, a Walker once lost here. He has forgotten his own name, but he knows this ward is "important." He stands here voluntarily, ensuring nothing disturbs this ward's memory.

- **If players respect his watch**: he tells them the tin "went down — toward the water." This points to Riverside Park (Stop C).
- **If players ignore him and enter the ward**: the room begins to "remember" the players are patients too. Players must pass a check to resist the space's suggestion ("You should be in bed. You're sick. You need rest.")

#### Scene Six: The Old Town's Old Street (Gap correspondence of Stop B)

*Follow the rust trail down from the hospital layer (or use a Shortcut — there is an emotional passage between the hospital's "sorrow" and the Old Town's "first love").*

*Atmosphere description*:
This is a double exposure of the 1960s and the present. The old street of the old woman's memory overlaps the skeleton of the modern street — wooden telephone poles grow up through the asphalt; an old-style bicycle leans against the door of a smartphone shop; the sign of an ice-parlor long vanished flickers in midair.

The sky is dusk — eternal dusk. The air of this street carries the scent of roasted sweet potatoes and some old-brand soap.

*Key elements*:
- An old theater — demolished on the Surface forty years ago. But in the Gap, the theater is still in business. The poster film is from 1963.
- In the ticket booth at the theater door sits a Mirror — a young Granny Lin (early twenties), waiting for someone. Every thirty seconds she turns to look at the street corner.
- Across from the theater — an ice-parlor. On a table inside the ice-parlor, a red tin box. The cookie tin.

*Lost Item Collector encounter*:
But the tin is not unguarded. A Lost Item Collector sits beside the tin — today it has collected "love letters." Its body is hung with envelopes, notes, and cards of every era.

- The Collector notices the players. It won't attack on its own — but it is especially interested in this tin.
- "This tin —" (the Collector's voice is many letter-papers rubbing together, overlapping) "— it came here on its own. I'm only watching it."
- The Collector is willing to let the players take the tin, but on a condition: it wants **one** letter from the tin. Not a specific one — the "most honest one." It asks the players to read the letters in the tin, and choose the most honest one to give it.
- If players agree and comply: reading the letters is a roleplay scene — players read aloud the letters Granny Lin wrote over sixty years. The GM should prepare 3–5 specific letters' contents. Players must choose. There is no correct answer — but the choice reveals the players' values.
- Whichever letter is chosen, the Collector carefully folds the letter-paper and places it in its own "collection." Then it says: "You know — this tin was meant to go farther. It doesn't want to be taken back. It wasn't lost — it walked away on its own."
- Then the Collector points toward the river. "It's looking for its other half. Those letters... they all flow in the same direction."

#### Scene Seven: The Riverside Memory Bench (Gap correspondence of Stop C)

*Toward the river, through ever-denser "memory density."*

*Atmosphere description*:
A river — but not of water. What flows in the river is... images. Fragments of memory. If you look closely, you can see a young couple walking hand in hand, a man on one knee, an old man sitting in the sunset.

On the riverbank is a bench. The bench faces the river. On the Surface, this bench is in Riverside Park, the old couple's favorite spot.

In the Gap, the space around the bench is compressed into eternal dusk. Dozens of Mirrors cycle here — the old couple at different ages, in different seasons, doing the same thing: sitting together.

*Key elements*:
- On the bench sits a Mirror — an elderly version of the husband. Not young, but old — he looks as he did on his last day. He is the image of the husband in the old woman's memory: gaunt but gentle-eyed.
- This Mirror's quality differs from the others — it is more "solid." Not only because the old woman remembers him. But because for forty years she passed here every day with those letters — her feelings "imprinted" him too deeply.
- When players approach, this Mirror turns to look at them. The other Mirrors all cycle — but he does not. He is simply waiting.

*The Husband's Mirror (special NPC)*:
- He does not speak — not that he cannot, but that he has nothing to say. He only wants to listen.
- If players take out a letter from the tin and read it to him — the Mirror closes its eyes. Not vanishing, but becoming calm.
- If players do not take out a letter — the Mirror extends a hand, palm up. A request.

*Information reveal*:
When players are in the bench scene, they notice the memory fragments in the river are not only the old woman's — but others'. This river is made of all "unspoken words." If players linger too long by the river, they begin to hear — not sounds, but their own memories' regrets surfacing. The progression of Gap Sickness accelerates here.

*Turning point*:
Beneath the bench, players find the "missing letter" from the tin — the letter Granny Lin wrote today, only half-finished: "I've always wanted to ask you..." The letter breaks off here.

But if players place this unfinished letter together with the husband's Mirror — if they set the letter on the bench and sit beside the Mirror — the letter completes itself. Not magic, but because the old woman has known the answer for forty years. She just didn't dare write it down.

The completed letter: "I've always wanted to ask you — the day you left, I held your hand, what was the last thing you wanted to say? I know you tried your best. But I've thought about it every night. Tonight I finally know the answer, don't I? What you wanted to say was: 'Thank you for coming every day.' Me too. Thank you for being there every day."

---

### Act Three: The Terminus — The Sky of Letters

#### Scene Eight: The Emotional Passage (deep in the Gap)

*Departing from the bench, following the guidance of the now "completed" letter.*

*Atmosphere description*:
Space begins to lose the disguise of buildings and streets. The players are entering "pure Gap" — no corresponding Surface structure. Around them is paper. Letter-paper — growing from the ground, floating in the air, forming walls and ceiling. Rivers of ink wind beneath their feet. Every sheet is filled with the old woman's handwriting.

This is not the two thousand letters in the tin — this is four thousand, six thousand. The old woman wrote letters all her life, starting while her husband was still alive. Some were never put in the tin. But they all flow here.

*Distance distortion*:
Here, distance is determined by "how well you understand the recipient." If you understand why the old woman wrote these letters — you move forward. If you don't — you stay in place.

#### Scene Nine: The Room of Paper and Ink (terminus)

*The end of the passage.*

*Atmosphere description*:
A room made of letters. The walls are layers of letter-paper, the floor is unfolded letters, the ceiling hangs with envelopes. In the center of the room sits an old man — not a Mirror, not a Lingerer. He is...

*GM options (choose one; do not tell players which)*:

**Option A**: He is the old woman's husband's "echo" in the Gap — not a soul, not a ghost, but the collective formed by forty years of letters. He has self-awareness, but knows he is not real. He waits because the letters point to him — every word of the old woman's sixty years found him in the Gap.

**Option B**: He is the Gap's own response to the old woman. The Gap remembers her — every night for forty years, the same route, the same time, the same seat. She is the most persistent person the Gap has ever seen. The Gap created this for her — not a husband, but "a place where goodbye can be said."

**Option C**: Nothing at all. The room is empty. But as players enter the room, each feels — a presence. Not visual, not auditory, but... as if someone just left. The chair still warm. A cup of tea on the table, not cold. In the air, a definite feeling: of being waited for, of being loved.

*Option C is recommended as default* — it best fits the Gap's philosophy (Evidence of Absence), and most engages players in constructing meaning.

#### Scene Ten: The Decision (terminus room)

Whichever option is chosen, the core of this scene is to let players make a decision:

*If Option C*:
- Players arrive at an empty room, but the room is full of evidence that "he just left."
- The most important discovery: on the table is a reply letter. In the old woman's husband's handwriting — though the old woman said he was illiterate (she taught him later).
- The letter is finished; all players hear it.
- They must now decide: bring this reply letter to Granny Lin? Or return the tin to the room, letting the old couple's correspondence continue in the Gap?

*Choice branches*:

**Bring back the tin and the reply**:
- Return to the Surface. See Granny Lin. Give her the tin and the reply.
- Granny Lin reads the reply. She cries — but after crying, she asks: "So I don't have to keep riding the last bus anymore?"
- Her ritual ends. Starting next week, she no longer rides the 47 last bus every night.
- But correspondingly — players notice, the Mirror on the Riverside Park bench has vanished. That space returns to its "normal" Gap state. The old couple's story ends.
- This is an ending. Not happy, not sad — complete.

**Leave the tin in the Gap**:
- Players decide not to take the tin. Granny Lin's letters keep flowing to that room.
- Return to the Surface. Granny Lin still has no tin. But players can tell her: "The tin is in a good place. Someone received it."
- Granny Lin is silent a moment. Then she laughs. "Then — I'll keep writing."
- Nothing changes. Yet everything changes. Because Granny Lin now knows — not believes, but knows — someone on the other side receives the letters.
- Players' reward: they find themselves with one more stable Threshold — the 47 last bus. As long as Granny Lin keeps writing, this Threshold stays open. This is an extremely rare gift.

**A third possibility** (if players think of it):
- Copy the reply. Bring the copy to Granny Lin, leave the original in the room.
- This is the closest to a "perfect" ending — but not free. Copying the reply requires a Memory Crystal as "paper." Players must sacrifice one of their own memories — a concrete, precious memory — to print the reply. That memory becomes blurry, like seeing through fog.

---

### Scenario Epilogue

Whatever the players choose, the ending of "The Last Bus" is not "mission complete." Rather:

- They witnessed a sixty-year-long conversation.
- They understood a truth: some things are in the Gap not because they were forgotten — but because they have not yet reached their destination.
- They left something on the route of the 47 last bus — perhaps a new Threshold, a memory, or simply the change that Granny Lin no longer needs to ride alone.

Final scene: The players return to the "Night Voyage" café. A-Kai is wiping a cup — the same cup. He looks up.

"So — is she alright?"

The players answer. Then outside the window, the 47 last bus passes. Its interior lit, empty.

Or — inside sits an old man, looking out the window, nodding slightly.

(GM's choice.)
